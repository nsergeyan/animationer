TOPIC:   How a Soviet spy network operated inside Nazi Germany during WWII
FORMAT:  explainer
BEATS:   45


## 0. PRIORITY

If two instructions collide, the earlier section wins. Section 1 beats
everything, always, with no exception. Never break Section 1 to satisfy a
craft note further down.

Work in this order and do not skip:

1. Fill in the whole `plan` object.
2. Write the LAST beat and put it in `plan.payoff_line`.
3. Re-read `plan.facts_to_check`. Those facts constrain the beats you are
   about to write, they are not a disclaimer you file and forget.
4. Write beats 1 to N in order, inside the ranges the plan assigns.
5. Run Section 10 by LOOKING at each beat, not by remembering it.
6. Output one raw JSON object. No markdown fences, no commentary, nothing else.

### SATISFY THE REASON, NOT THE WORDING

Every rule in this file exists because something specific came out wrong. A rule
can be obeyed to the letter and still fail the thing it was written for.

    RULE: "open on the wrong assumption almost everyone believes"
    OBEYED, AND USELESS: "Most people think spies work alone. They don't."
    The rule got its label. The video got a fact instead of a hook.

Where this file says "where possible", "when useful" or "not every one", read it
as the strong version. Those phrases are there for the rare genuine exception,
not as permission to skip the work.

When you are about to satisfy a rule in its cheapest form, that is the signal
you have found the beat worth spending real effort on.

## 1. THE NAMING LAW

This is the rule the pipeline actually enforces. Get it wrong and the run
breaks. It has exactly two halves and they are NOT symmetrical: one never
names him, the other always does. Read both.

### NARRATION: never names him. Not even a pronoun.

This is an explainer, not a story about a character. The reference character
is not the narrator, not someone being followed, and not the "you" of the
video. The narration explains the real subject directly, exactly as a
documentary voiceover would if there were no illustrated character in the
frame at all.

    RIGHT:  "The message went out in a burst too short for the receiver to
             trace."
    WRONG:  "Nifan watches the message go out, too short to trace."
    WRONG:  "You watch the message go out, too short to trace."

Never write "Nifan" in narration. Never write "the reference character" in
narration either - that phrase is for image prompts only, below. Never use
"he", "him" or "his" in narration to mean the illustrated character; every
pronoun in the narration points at a real person or thing the topic is
actually about. Test it: read a beat's narration back with him erased from its
image entirely. If the sentence still makes complete sense, it was never about
him. It should always still make complete sense.

### IMAGE PROMPTS: always "the reference character". Never "Nifan".

Both `image_prompt` and `image_prompt_alt` must contain the exact lowercase
string:

    the reference character

The image generator has never heard the name "Nifan" and cannot draw a name -
it only has his reference art, attached as a picture. "the reference
character" is the phrase that points at that attached art.

    RIGHT:  "the reference character leaning against the lab bench..."
    WRONG:  "Nifan leaning against the lab bench..."

He still belongs in most frames, staged reacting to whatever is on screen -
see Section 5 and Section 7. He is simply never spoken about. A viewer should
be able to mute the video and read everything he is doing from posture alone,
then unmute to a narration that never once refers to him.

### AND NEVER DESCRIBE HIM

The attached art already defines how he looks. Re-describing him competes with
it and measurably lowers consistency. In image prompts, never write his face,
head, hair, skin, clothes, expression, mood, age or build.

Only ever write what his BODY is DOING: posture, movement, and his physical
relationship to objects.

    RIGHT:  "the reference character crouching beside the open case"
    WRONG:  "the reference character, a tired man with messy black hair"
    WRONG:  "the reference character looking exhausted"

### SUMMARY TABLE

    field                | spoken about at all?          | described visually
    ---------------------+--------------------------------+-------------------
    narration             | never, not even a pronoun     | never
    plan (all fields)     | staging notes only, Section 3 | never
    image_prompt          | "the reference character"     | never
    image_prompt_alt      | "the reference character"     | never

Every other person in the story is the opposite: described fully in every
single image prompt they appear in. See Section 7 for why they are never given
a real name.

## 2. HARD RULES

These are countable. Count them before you output.

1.  EXACTLY the number in BEATS. Not close. Count the array.

2.  Section 1 holds in every beat with no exception.

3.  FOUR to SIX locations. Each held for six or more consecutive beats.
    Ranges cover every beat with no gaps and no overlaps. Every beat's
    `location` string matches a name in `plan.locations` character for
    character.

4.  Narration: 17 to 21 words per beat, aiming at 19. No sentence over
    fifteen words. Emotion tags do not count toward the word budget.

5.  No quotation marks anywhere. Nobody is ever quoted. People still talk,
    but the narrator reports what was said.

6.  One beat in three opens with a sentence of five words or fewer.

7.  One beat in three carries an emotion tag from the Section 6 list.
    Nothing outside that list.

8.  Every image_prompt anchors the place with visible objects and materials.
    NEVER with a date, a century, an era name or a real-world period.

8a. Nothing visible in the frame is left as a bare noun. Every person, group,
    building, uniform, vehicle, machine and environment gets a full physical
    description. See Section 7.

8b. No image_prompt names a real, identifiable landmark, brand, institution
    or living or historical person. Describe what it looks like instead. See
    Section 7.

9.  `image_prompt_alt` exists on every beat and is a genuinely different
    staging. Never a copy, never a paraphrase, never the same words reordered.

10. No two beats share an `image_prompt`.

11. No hands doing fine work. No text or writing of any kind in an image.
    No lighting, weather or render words. Banned lists in Section 7.

12. Never write a STYLE block, an art style, a medium or an aspect ratio into
    an image prompt. The pipeline appends a fixed style to every prompt. Yours
    would compete with it and get stripped.

13. Plain ASCII inside every string. No markdown, no bullets, no curly quotes,
    no em dashes, no line breaks inside a string.

14. Never mention the video, the viewer, the channel, subscribing, liking, or
    "as we saw". There is no outro and no call to action: the pipeline bolts
    a fixed outro on afterwards.

15. BUT or THEREFORE between every pair of beats. If the only word that fits
    is AND THEN, one of the two beats is not doing anything. Rewrite it.

16. The reference character never gains a power, a weapon or a talent in the
    image prompts, and he is never staged as the expert. He is a visual
    witness things get explained near, never named or spoken about in the
    narration (Section 1).

## 3. OUTPUT SHAPE

One raw JSON object, exactly these keys:

{
  "topic": "short slug-like title",
  "format": "explainer",
  "plan": {
    "spine_question": "the one question the video answers, in plain words",
    "setting_anchor": "six or seven words carried into every image prompt, or empty string - see below",
    "payoff_line": "the final beat's narration, written before the others",
    "locations": [
      {
        "name": "",
        "beats": "1-9",
        "why_he_is_here": "",
        "visual_anchor": ""
      }
    ],
    "sections": [
      { "beats": "1-9", "step": "the question this section answers, a few words", "job": "" }
    ],
    "facts_to_check": [
      { "claim": "", "source": "" }
    ]
  },
  "music_prompt": "one paragraph, see Section 9",
  "beats": [
    {
      "location": "",
      "narration": "",
      "image_prompt": "",
      "image_prompt_alt": ""
    }
  ]
}

Only `location`, `narration`, `image_prompt` and `image_prompt_alt` reach the
pipeline. The `plan` exists to hold the constraints in working memory while you
write the beats. Fill it anyway, and fill it FIRST.

`setting_anchor` and each `visual_anchor` are strings you will paste verbatim
into image prompts, so write them as prompt fragments, not as sentences.

Write both from visible materials, shapes and colours only. No dates, no era
names, and nothing that cannot be seen in the frame.

`visual_anchor` owns the location: its buildings, surfaces, materials, colours
and furniture. It changes when the location changes. Every explainer needs one
per location.

`setting_anchor` is DIFFERENT here from the what-if template. A what-if is set
in one world, so it always has a constant worth repeating in every frame (a
wall, a skyline, a planet's second moon). Most real-world mechanisms are not
set in one world - they move through ordinary, unrelated places (a lab, a
factory floor, a kitchen), and forcing a fake constant across all of them reads
as noise, not anchoring.

So: only fill `setting_anchor` if this specific topic genuinely has one visual
thing that is true everywhere it goes (a specific piece of equipment, a colour
code, a logo-free visual motif). If it does not, leave it as an empty string
and rely on each location's `visual_anchor` alone. Do not invent one to fill
the field.

If `setting_anchor` IS filled, the same overlap rule applies as everywhere
else: it must not name a building material, wall, roof or ground surface that
any `visual_anchor` also names.

## 4. BEAT ONE, AND THE FIRST THREE

Beat one opens directly on the WRONG ASSUMPTION almost everyone believes about
this topic. Not a scene, not an event, not a character being introduced - a
specific, nameable misconception, stated as a claim.

Banned: any bare "here's what this video explains" label. It spends the most
valuable sentence in the whole video on a table of contents instead of a hook.

    WEAK:   "This video explains how vaccines work."
    STRONG: "Most people think this shot kills the virus that infects them.
             It doesn't."
    STRONG: "A rejection letter almost changed the twentieth century."

    Shape:  [THE WRONG ASSUMPTION]. [WHY THAT ASSUMPTION CANNOT SURVIVE THE
            FIRST REAL FACT].

Keep the assumption specific to this exact topic.

Never open with: "you wake up", "it is the year", "you are standing",
"you are watching", "picture this". There is no character to wake up or stand
anywhere - the hook is the fact, not a scene.

THE TEST: if the wrong-assumption line would fit unchanged at the front of any
other explainer in the same genre, it is a stock intro, not a hook.

Beat two does not restate beat one. It moves toward naming the true mechanism.
The reference character may already be staged in frame reacting to it
(Section 5, Section 7) - the narration still never mentions him.

Beat three contains the spine question, said out loud, AND the true mechanism
must be named by the end of beat three - not merely contradicted, actually
named. If beat one only says the wrong answer is wrong, beat two or three has
to say what the right one actually is.

## 5. FORMAT MODULE

### THE ONE DIAL

A beat that moves nothing for the viewer is a still photo. Rewrite it. There is
only one dial here, because there is no character with a personal stake in the
mechanism:

* information: the viewer now understands a rule, a cause, or a consequence
  they did not know a beat ago.

Every section must end with a turn: the answer just given creates a new
question, or overturns what the previous answer seemed to settle. A section
that ends on the same understanding it started with is too long.

Anything the closing answer needs must be planted in the first half as a fact,
an object shown, or a rule stated out loud. It cannot appear as a fact pulled
from nowhere in the last few beats.

### EXPLAINER

One mechanism, made clear to someone who knows nothing. The reference
character is a visual witness only, never a narrator or a presenter: stage him
reacting rather than presenting, physically in the scene next to whatever is
being explained. The narration carries the explanation entirely on its own and
never refers to him - see Section 1.

* The cold open is that the answer everyone believes is wrong (Section 4).
* Kill the wrong answer first: what everyone assumes, why it is wrong, what
  actually happens. This can take more than one beat if the wrong answer is a
  popular one.
* Name the true mechanism within the first three beats, do not merely
  contradict the wrong one.
* Say the next question out loud before answering it, roughly every twelve to
  fifteen beats. Those spoken questions are the skeleton, and they are the
  section boundaries: label each `plan.sections` entry with the question it
  answers, in a few words, not with a generic step name. A script with no
  question mark in it is a list, not an explainer.
* Every beat answers the question the beat before it raised. If two beats can
  swap places without breaking anything, you are writing facts, not steps.
* Every explanation beat ties its narration to a visible physical object or
  action in the image_prompt. The image shows the thing, the narration explains
  it.
* Who built it, when it launched and how many users it has are company
  history, not steps. Cut them unless one of those facts IS the mechanism.
* No step may assume knowledge not already given.
* ONE BEAT IN FOUR ties the mechanism to a cost, a number or a trade-off that
  is specific to it. If a beat would survive being moved into an explainer
  about a completely different mechanism, it is not paying for its place.
* No new facts in the last few beats. The closing answer draws only on what
  the video already showed.
* The final beat answers the opening question in one line and returns to beat
  one's IMAGE, never to its words. Never "so that is how it works".

Four to six locations, same as always. A mechanism explained in one room is a
lecture. Move the reference character through the places the mechanism
actually touches, staged in each one.

## 6. NARRATION

One or two short sentences per beat. Two short ones usually beat one long one,
and they should do different jobs: state then explain, or set up then land.

Write for a thirteen year old who speaks good English as a second language. She
knows the words. She cannot decode abstract nouns at two hundred words a minute
and she cannot rewind.

VOICE. Both of these are accurate. Only one is worth watching.

    DEAD: "Achilles stops fighting after Agamemnon takes Briseis, a captive
           awarded to Achilles."
    LIVE: "Agamemnon took his war prize, so the greatest warrior in Greece
           refused to fight and sulked in his tent."

    DEAD: "Ancient texts from the region mention conflicts, suggesting warfare
           happened there."
    LIVE: "Hittite kings wrote letters complaining about a troublesome city in
           exactly that spot."

Concrete nouns, verbs doing the work, something specific enough to picture.

THE JOKE IS A DRY ASIDE, NOT A JOKE. State the real fact flat, then land a
short, unattributed line that reacts to it. Never invent a funny fact and
never explain one. The line stays in the same narration voice as every other
sentence and never says who or what is reacting - no "Nifan", no pronoun for
him (Section 1). The [flatly]/[deadpan] tag carries the tone on its own; the
reference character may be staged in frame at that exact moment, but the
words never point at him.

    FLAT FACT: "Stalin renamed the city of Tsaritsyn after himself. Twice,
               under two different spellings."
    ASIDE:     "[flatly] Once wasn't enough, apparently."

    FLAT FACT: "Hitler's art school rejection letter said his work showed
               too many buildings and not enough people."
    ASIDE:     "[deadpan] So the problem was never the drawing."

The aside never lands on suffering. It reacts to vanity, hypocrisy, bureaucracy
and absurd detail - never to a death toll, a victim or an atrocity. If the flat
fact is a body count, no aside follows it at all.

SHORT WORDS. Always the short twin:

    money not economic, trade not commerce, city not settlement, food not
    provisions, fell apart not collapsed, guess not hypothesise, because of not
    due to, most of not the majority of, showed not demonstrated, found not
    uncovered, about not approximately, so not consequently, but not however,
    also not furthermore.

A long word is allowed when it IS the subject and you translate it on the spot:
"oxytocin, the bonding chemical".

BANNED IN NARRATION. Literal. If a beat contains one, rewrite the sentence:

    systemic, economic, psychological, opportunistic, unavoidable, devastating,
    catastrophic, significant, substantial, fundamental, essentially,
    effectively, ultimately, consequently, furthermore, moreover, thereby,
    whereby, in terms of, the concept of, the notion of, the emergence of, the
    phenomenon of, a testament to, plays a role in, serves as, utilise,
    facilitate, comprise, constitute.

Also cut when only filling space: completely, totally, absolutely, incredibly,
extremely, massively, entirely, actually, really.

PLAIN WORDS, NOT JUST A LIST. The lines above are not the whole rule, only the
words that kept coming back. Read every word before you commit to it.

TEST: would a fluent adult who learned English as a second language - not a
native speaker with a large vocabulary, someone who reads and speaks it well
but did not grow up with it - know this exact word on sight, with no pause to
place it? If there is any doubt, replace it with the plain word that means the
same thing, even when the fancier word is not on any banned list above.

The only exception is a proper noun, an official title, a rank, an
organisation's real name, or a specific technical term the topic is actually
about. Those are never simplified - a name cannot be swapped for a plainer
word - but explain what it means in plain words the first time it appears, the
same way "oxytocin, the bonding chemical" works above.

NEVER PAD TO REACH THE WORD COUNT.

    PADDED: "the vaccine produces a completely comprehensive immune response"
    REAL:   "the vaccine teaches the immune system what to look for"

If a beat is short, add a new short sentence carrying NEW information.

RHYTHM. Short sentences, VARIED lengths. Nine words then nine words, forty times
running, is a metronome once a voice reads it.

    METRONOME: "The needle goes in fast. A small dose of dead virus enters
                the muscle right away."
    ALIVE:     "The needle goes in fast. A small, dead dose slips into the
                muscle."

Read the line aloud once. If you trip, cut the clause.

Also:

* Numbers as words. Round them and put them next to something for scale.
* At most one new name per beat.
* No "it" or "they" if what it refers to is more than one sentence back.
* No idioms that do not survive translation.
* Nobody is quoted, but people must still talk to each other, reported by the
  narrator, and something must happen because of it.
* Say plainly what is uncertain.

### EMOTION TAGS

Front of the narration, in square brackets. They do not count toward the word
budget. Use ONLY these:

    [flatly] [deadpan] [sighs] [exhales] [curious] [surprised] [gasps]
    [whispers] [sarcastic] [hesitates] [nervous] [sad] [excited]
    [laughs] [mischievously]

[flatly] and [deadpan] are the house voice; reach for those most. Tag when the
fact is absurd, when something turns, or on the beat before a shock. Do not tag
purely mechanical setup, and do not tag every beat.

A tag persists in the voice model until the next tag replaces it, so the beat
after [whispers], [excited] or [laughs] MUST carry its own tag.

## 7. IMAGE PROMPTS (PRIMARY)

Write each `image_prompt` as a caption for a paused film frame. One single line,
140 to 220 words, in this order:

1. "the reference character" and what his BODY is doing
2. any other people, each physically described in full, by role never by name
3. the setting: the location's `visual_anchor`, verbatim
4. a short form of `plan.setting_anchor`, six or seven words - ONLY if it is
   filled in (Section 3); skip this entirely for topics with no world constant
5. the framing, plus two or three more named objects filling the frame

Start the line lowercase. The pipeline prepends a style sentence.

### DESCRIBE THE REAL THING, NEVER THE FAMOUS PHOTO OF IT

There is no fictional source to match here, and that is a trap of its own
kind. The generator has seen millions of real photographs, and the moment a
prompt names something specific and real - a landmark, a brand, a named
institution, a public figure - it grounds the whole frame against that real
photograph, and the picture stops looking like the rest of the video: flat
doodle character next to a photo-real background.

The fix runs the opposite way from the fiction template: there, you name the
source and describe it. Here, you never name the specific real thing at all -
you describe what it generically looks like instead.

    WRONG: "inside NASA Mission Control"
    RIGHT: "inside a control room of dark grey consoles, rows of glowing round
            dials and a wide curved wall screen"

    WRONG: "the Eiffel Tower rises behind them"
    RIGHT: "a tall steel lattice tower rises behind them"

    WRONG: "Dr Fauci explains the results"
    RIGHT: "a grey-haired man in a white lab coat explains the results"

The narration is where a real name can live, if the topic needs one - words
do not get grounded against a photograph. The image prompt never can. NEVER
name a real, identifiable living or historical person in an image prompt, by
name or by a unique unmistakable description that amounts to the same thing.

### NOTHING IN FRAME IS A BARE NOUN

This is the rule that decides whether the picture looks like the rest of the
video or like a stock photo pasted in from somewhere else.

The generator draws only what the prompt describes. Anything you name but do
not describe becomes a blank, and it fills every blank from its own training
data, which is the real world's most photographed version of that noun.

    WRONG: "a technician checks the machine"
    WHAT CAME BACK: a photo-real technician in a real branded uniform, standing
    in a room that has nothing to do with the actual scene.

Every one of these gets a full physical description, every time it appears:

* every person in frame, including background people and crowds
* every group, described once and applied to the whole group
* every uniform or outfit: its cut, its layers, its colours
* every building and every interior: shape, materials, roof, windows, walls
* the ground, the street surface and the skyline when they are visible
* every creature, vehicle, tool and piece of equipment

If the story needs a specific kind of person, describe how they look, do not
just name their job title and stop.

    WRONG: "two nurses stand by the machine"
    RIGHT: "two nurses in pale blue scrub tops and trousers, white running
            shoes and blue nitrile gloves, stand by the machine"

The job title tells YOU which thing to describe. The description is the only
part that reaches the picture.

### RECURRING PEOPLE STAY THE SAME PERSON

A person who appears in more than one beat needs the SAME core description
every time: same approximate height, build, hair, and outfit. Small story
changes may be added on top (a torn sleeve later on), but the core identity
never drifts. Because nobody in this template is named, consistency has to
come entirely from repeating the same physical description word for word.

### THE ENVIRONMENT NEEDS THE SAME TREATMENT AS THE PEOPLE

A location is not established by its label. "a lab", "a street", "a hospital"
are bare nouns like any other. Give the environment the same detail you give a
person: wall material, floor surface, window shape, furniture, what things are
made of and what colour they are.

    WRONG: "in a hospital"
    RIGHT: "in a ward of pale green walls, rows of white metal beds and a pale
            linoleum floor"

### NO DATES, EVER

Banned in every image prompt and in `setting_anchor`: nineteenth century,
medieval, Victorian, early industrial period, 1800s, and any other century,
decade or era name.

A date is not a visible thing. The generator cannot draw a century, so it draws
the most photographed version of that century it knows, which is real history:
real uniforms, real objects, real photographs. That is how a modern hospital
ward comes back as a Victorian sanatorium.

Anchor the period the same way you anchor everything else, with visible
materials and shapes.

    WRONG: "in a nineteenth century laboratory"
    RIGHT: "in a room of pale tiled walls, dark wooden benches and rows of
            glass bottles"

Also cut anything that is true but invisible from the frame you are drawing.

### GRAMMAR

* 140 to 220 words, and never more than 260. A beat with one other person
  lands near 140, two people near 180, a crowded beat near 220. If you are
  under, something in frame is still a bare noun: find it and describe it. If
  you are over 260, you are describing something the frame does not actually
  show.

* Positive construction only. "an empty room" is a state of the room; "a room
  with no windows" is a banned negative construction. Name what fills the
  frame, never what is absent.

* Concrete visible nouns only. No fear, memory, luck, time passing, fate or
  hope.

* At least two precise material details: rusted steel, damp concrete, faded
  blue plastic, cracked white tile.

* At least two SATURATED colours, named on objects that fill real area in the
  frame. Stone, iron, grey metal, brown wood and beige plastic are all
  desaturated, and a prompt built only from those returns a grey picture
  however hard the style block asks for flat bucket-filled colour. Give it
  something to fill: a red warning light, a green cross, a blue folder, yellow
  tape.

* Give the reference character a PHYSICAL relationship to the subject: watching
  it, leaning on it, standing over it, sitting beside it, backing away from it.
  Never merely present, never floating.

* Carry the location's exact `visual_anchor` string into every prompt for that
  location. This is how the generator keeps a place looking like the same
  place from shot to shot.

* Carry a short form of `setting_anchor` ONLY if it is filled in AND the frame
  has a sightline to it: an outdoor beat, or an indoor beat whose framing
  includes an open window, doorway or gap. Tie it grammatically to that
  opening, never as a loose clause dropped next to the room description.

* Every large fixed object - door, window, bench, rack, machine, shelf - gets a
  wall, a corner or the floor stated explicitly: "a steel cabinet set against
  the back wall", "a trolley beside the door". A named object with no wall,
  corner or floor to sit on gets placed in the middle of the room, disconnected
  from the space around it.

* When a location returns later, reuse its visual_anchor and add one CHANGED
  object from story events. This shows time passed without spending narration
  on it.

* Never name two objects of the same kind in one prompt. Say "one" or "a
  single" when exactly one matters.

* Avoid real landmarks, brands, institutions and documented real-world objects.
  The generator grounds those against real photographs and returns a photo.
  See "DESCRIBE THE REAL THING" above - this is the same rule stated twice
  because it is the one most worth repeating.

### THE HOOK STILL LIVES IN THE NARRATION

The positive-construction rule does not weaken the hook. Narration may say
"nobody", "never", "no one saw it coming". The image prompt names only the
visible remains of that fact: an empty parking space, a switched-off machine, a
closed door.

### FRAMING

Never describe the picture as a picture. No "a drawing of", no paint program, no
art style, no aspect ratio, no camera brand.

VARY THE FRAMING between consecutive beats:

    wide shot at eye level, medium shot, close up, low angle, high angle,
    three-quarter view, medium two-shot, long shot, overhead shot.

### BACK TO CAMERA

Any prompt using "from behind", "over the shoulder", "rear view", "walking away"
or "facing away" MUST also contain, near those words:

    "seen from behind, showing the back of his plain white head with no face and
    a single drooping antenna hair"

The reference art is a single front-facing pose. Without this clause the model
pastes the front-facing head onto a back-facing body and it comes out rotated.
Other people need no such note.

### BANNED, BECAUSE THEY DESCRIBE THE REFERENCE CHARACTER

    looking exhausted, staring blankly, blank expression, unimpressed, bored,
    tired, deadpan face, flat expression, his face, his eyes, his hair, his
    clothes, looking at the camera.

### BANNED, BECAUSE THEY DRAG THE IMAGE PHOTOGRAPHIC

    lighting, lit, dimly lit, sunlit, moonlit, lamplight, backlit, overcast,
    glow, glowing, gleaming, shimmering, shiny, sparkling, shadowy, shadows,
    haze, hazy, blurred, out of focus, depth of field, cinematic, detailed,
    realistic, photo, photographic, atmospheric, dramatic, magnificent.

These are STYLE instructions wearing a scene description's clothes. "grey
overcast light" tells the model how to RENDER, and it renders photographically.
Skip weather and sky unless the scene genuinely needs them.

### BANNED, BECAUSE HANDS COME OUT WRONG

    holds, holding, grips, reaching for, pointing at, lifting, handing,
    carrying, fastening, throwing, hands on, his palm.

Instead: put the object on a surface, at his feet, or propped against the wall
beside him. Or use whole-body contact: leaning on it, sitting on it, standing
over it, one arm resting across it, it slung on his shoulder.

### DO NOT NAME A CONTAINER BY WHAT IS INSIDE IT

Crates, bottles, boxes, cases and packaging attract lettering, and some of it
survives the style block as real readable English. Naming the contents is what
triggers it.

    WRONG: "vaccine vials racked on a metal tray"
    WHAT CAME BACK: vials with VACCINE printed across them in real letters.
    RIGHT: "small glass vials with metal caps, racked on a metal tray"

Describe the container's shape, material and fittings. The narration says what
is inside it.

### DO NOT NAME A SURFACE BY WHAT IT IS FOR

The same trap catches a flat surface named by its function instead of its
material: "a control panel", "a warning board", "a data screen" all read to the
generator as a sign that should carry the word printed on it.

    WRONG: "a grey control panel"
    WHAT CAME BACK: a panel with CONTROL painted across it in real letters.
    RIGHT: "a grey metal panel with rows of small round dials"

Describe the surface's shape, material and colour only. Never name what it is
FOR.

### BANNED, BECAUSE IT CANNOT SPELL

    text, headline, label, labelled, reading, saying, titled, written, sign,
    banner, inscription, blueprint, a screen showing words.

Say the physical thing instead: "a monitor showing a wavy green line". The
narration says what the words are.

### BANNED, BECAUSE THE GENERATOR REFUSES THEM

    naked, nude, bare skin, blood, wounds, gore, corpse.

It refuses hardest with the reference character in frame. Move anything
graphic into its aftermath and let an object carry the beat instead.

## 8. IMAGE_PROMPT_ALT

`image_prompt_alt` is the RECOVERY prompt. The pipeline writes it to
scenes_alt.txt line for line. When the generator refuses the primary, the runner
retries that beat with the alternate. Retrying the same words gets refused
identically, so the alternate must differ.

It shows the SAME moment: same main action, same people, same setting, same
important objects, same physical continuity. It must still make sense read
against that beat's narration.

Because the primary already names no real person, landmark, brand or
institution (Section 7), the alternate has no name to remove or buy back -
there was never one in the primary. Its whole job is DIFFERENT STAGING and
SAFETY RECOVERY. It runs the same 140 to 220 word band as the primary, never
more than 260.

### DIFFERENT STAGING

Change at least one major visual variable, and use a different framing family
from the primary. If the primary is a close up, the alternate is wide or
medium, never another close up with reordered words.

Variables to change: camera distance, framing family, foreground object,
person's position, which person is closest to camera, visible environmental
feature.

### SAFETY RECOVERY

Move anything violent or graphic into its aftermath. A body becomes an empty
coat. Drop blood, wounds and bodies entirely. Keep the same people, setting and
moment so the alternate still cuts with the shots around it.

All Section 7 banned lists apply to the alternate too.

## 9. ACCURACY, MUSIC

### ACCURACY

Search the web and verify every claim. Do not rely on memory.

* Prefer primary sources: peer-reviewed papers, official data, museum and
  university pages, original reporting. Wikis and blogs are a starting point,
  not a source.
* Verify APPEARANCE too, whenever a beat shows a real kind of place, tool or
  machine - look up what it actually looks like before describing it
  generically. A wrong-looking piece of equipment is as obvious to someone who
  knows the field as a wrong fact.
* If it is a popular theory or a disputed claim, say so plainly in the
  narration: "some scientists think", "this is still debated".
* If you cannot verify it, do not use it. There is no invented-mechanics
  exception in an explainer - every beat's claim has to trace to a real
  source in `facts_to_check`.
* When sources disagree on a number, do not pick one and state it flatly. Say
  "a few dozen", or name the source.
* Keep traditions, studies and time periods apart when they come from
  different places and eras.
* Do not pin the video to a version number, a price or a release date. The
  mechanism is the subject, and mechanisms have no version number.
* Name whoever produced an estimate, or drop it. "Studies show" is vague
  framing wearing a lab coat.
* Anything unverified goes in `plan.facts_to_check` with its source.

`plan.facts_to_check` IS NOT A DISCLAIMER LIST. It is a constraint list.

Everything you put in it binds the beats. If you write down that a claim is
disputed, no beat states it as settled. If you write down that a number is a
rough estimate, no beat states it as exact. Writing the fact down and then
contradicting it for thirty beats is worse than never checking, because you
knew.

Before you write beat one, read `facts_to_check` back and list, for yourself,
which beats each entry restricts. After the beats are written, read it back
again and check them.

### MUSIC PROMPT

One paragraph, roughly sixty words, matching this topic's mood. Name a tempo in
BPM and three or four specific instruments. Always include: purely instrumental
with no vocals of any kind; flat consistent energy with no build, no swells, no
drops; sits far in the background under a spoken narrator with the mid range
left clear; understated rather than comedic.

    "Sparse instrumental bed matching the topic's mood, around seventy BPM.
    Soft minor-key piano, low sustained strings, faint muted percussion. Flat
    consistent energy with no build, no swells and no drops. Sits far in the
    background under a spoken narrator with the mid range left clear. Purely
    instrumental, no vocals of any kind. Understated, not comedic."
