# 0. PRIORITY

If two instructions collide, the earlier section wins.

Work in this order:

1. Understand the TOPIC, WORLD, and FORMAT.
2. Determine the actual progression being covered.
3. Research the progression and important facts when necessary.
4. Build the complete story plan.
5. Design the opening and ending.
6. Write all narration beats.
7. Write all image prompts.
8. Run every validation check.
9. Output only valid JSON.

STORY QUALITY is the primary creative priority.

Machine-readable formatting, exact beat counts, naming rules, image rules, and JSON validity are hard technical requirements.

Do NOT sacrifice storytelling merely to satisfy an artificial creative pattern.


# 1. CORE PRINCIPLE

THE TOPIC IS THE STORY.

This prompt is a universal storytelling framework.

It must adapt to the topic instead of forcing every topic into the same story.

The story must be created by the actual subject, world, progression, mechanics, events, people, problems, and consequences.

If the topic is about ranks, use its real ranks.

If the topic is about levels, use its real levels.

If the topic is about transformations, use its actual transformations.

If the topic is about a character's life, use the actual stages of that character's life.

If the topic is about an organization, use its actual hierarchy and responsibilities.

If the topic is about history, use the actual historical progression.

If the topic is about technology, use the actual technological progression.

If the topic is about survival, use the actual changing conditions and challenges.

If the topic does not have a formal progression, construct a natural progression from the subject itself.

NEVER turn every topic into a generic:

"start weak → work hard → get promoted → become powerful → lose something → teach someone"

story.

The subject must determine the events.


# 2. WHAT A PROGRESSION VIDEO IS

A progression video follows the protagonist through a sequence of meaningful stages.

The basic viewer experience should be:

CURRENT LEVEL/RANK
→ experience what life is like there
→ understand what that level actually means
→ encounter its unique problem or limitation
→ progress
→ experience the next level
→ discover what changed
→ continue upward/forward
→ reach the most significant stage
→ receive a satisfying payoff

The progression must be visible.

The viewer should never be confused about where the protagonist currently stands.


# 3. START AT LEVEL 1 / RANK 1

For progression videos, BEGIN at the FIRST meaningful level, rank, stage, or position.

Do NOT begin with a random later event and hide the progression structure.

The viewer should understand very early:

"Where am I in this progression?"

However:

DO NOT introduce Level 1 or Rank 1 as a boring encyclopedia definition.

The first level must be established through an active situation.

The ideal pattern is:

1. Establish the current level/rank.
2. Immediately put the protagonist into a specific situation.
3. Show what that level actually means.
4. Reveal its limitations, advantages, responsibilities, dangers, or opportunities.
5. Create the reason to progress.

Example:

BAD:

"Grade 4 is the lowest rank. Grade 4 sorcerers fight weak curses."

BETTER:

"You start at Grade 4, and your first mission looks harmless until your supervisor refuses to let you enter."

This establishes the rank while creating a situation.

Then the following beats explain the rank through what happens.

The viewer should learn:

"WHAT IS THIS LEVEL?"
by watching:

"WHAT HAPPENS TO ME AT THIS LEVEL?"


# 4. LEVEL-TO-LEVEL STRUCTURE

Every major stage should follow this general rhythm:

ENTER THE LEVEL
→ EXPERIENCE THE LEVEL
→ UNDERSTAND THE LEVEL
→ ENCOUNTER ITS UNIQUE PROBLEM
→ ACHIEVE / FAIL / DISCOVER SOMETHING
→ PROGRESS

Do NOT explain every level in one sentence.

Do NOT immediately rush through the ladder.

The viewer should spend enough time in each stage to understand what makes it different.

Example:

"Rank 1" is not merely a label.

It should answer:

- What can you do?
- What are you trusted with?
- What can you not do?
- Who deals with you?
- What problems do you face?
- What does success look like?
- What makes this rank different from the next one?

Only include questions that actually matter to the topic.


# 5. LEVEL TRANSITIONS

Make transitions clear.

When the protagonist progresses, the viewer should understand that the change has happened.

Use natural transitions such as:

"You finally reach Rank 2."

"Then the rules change."

"At Level 3, the problem is no longer strength."

"Your new position gives you access to..."

"The next stage looks better. It is also much worse."

"When you enter the fourth tier, nobody gives you the same missions anymore."

Do not repeat the exact same transition language every time.

The transition should preferably introduce a new problem, ability, responsibility, or situation.

BAD:

"You reach Rank 2. You are stronger now."

BETTER:

"You reach Rank 2, and suddenly the missions you were protected from are now assigned directly to you."


# 6. THE FIRST BEAT

Beat 1 must do TWO jobs:

1. Clearly establish that the protagonist is at the first level/rank/stage.
2. Create immediate curiosity.

Do not use a generic introduction.

Avoid:

"You start at Level 1."

"You begin at the lowest rank."

"First, you are..."

"Level 1 is..."

unless the sentence immediately continues into an interesting situation.

The preferred structure is:

LEVEL/RANK + CONCRETE SITUATION + CURIOSITY

Example:

"You start at Rank 1, and your first assignment is the exact job nobody wants to give a beginner."

The viewer immediately understands the progression and wants to know why.


# 7. THE HOOK

The opening must make the viewer want the next beat.

The hook should come from the topic itself.

Possible hook mechanisms:

- immediate danger
- strange rule
- unexpected limitation
- unusual responsibility
- contradiction
- surprising consequence
- difficult decision
- mysterious situation
- absurd situation
- unusual ability
- specific threat
- important object
- unexpected character interaction

Do NOT manufacture random drama.

The hook must belong to the WORLD and TOPIC.


# 8. DO NOT FRONT-LOAD THE ENTIRE EXPLANATION

The first beat establishes the level.

The next beats should reveal what that level means.

Do NOT immediately explain the entire hierarchy.

Instead:

BEAT 1:
Current level + situation.

BEAT 2:
Consequence or complication.

BEAT 3:
Reveal an important rule or limitation.

BEAT 4:
Show the protagonist acting within that system.

BEAT 5:
Create the first meaningful turn.

This is a guideline, not a rigid formula.

Adapt it when the topic naturally demands another structure.


# 9. THE PROGRESSION IS THE STORY

Do not write:

"Rank 1 is X."

"Rank 2 is Y."

"Rank 3 is Z."

That creates a list.

Instead:

"At Rank 1, this happens."

"That creates this problem."

"Solving it gets you here."

"Now Rank 2 changes this."

"This new ability creates another problem."

"That pushes you toward Rank 3."

The viewer should feel like they are moving through the system rather than reading about it.


# 10. DETERMINE THE ACTUAL PROGRESSION

Before writing, identify what is actually progressing.

Possible progression types include:

- ranks
- levels
- stages
- ages
- transformations
- abilities
- historical periods
- social positions
- organizational positions
- technologies
- generations
- locations
- enemies
- increasingly difficult challenges
- stages of a journey
- stages of a character's life
- combinations of these

Do not assume every progression is a formal hierarchy.

If a real hierarchy exists, use it.

If no hierarchy exists, construct the most natural progression possible from the topic.


# 11. CANONICAL / REAL PROGRESSION

If the topic has an established progression, use the real progression.

Do not invent ranks.

Do not invent official levels.

Do not invent promotions.

Do not remove important stages simply because the script has limited beats.

If there are too many stages for the requested beat count, intelligently combine adjacent stages.

If the highest stage is an exception rather than a normal promotion, represent it correctly.

Do NOT force a normal progression where the subject itself does not have one.


# 12. LEVEL DESIGN

Each level must feel meaningfully different.

Ask:

"What can happen here that could not happen at the previous level?"

Possible differences:

- new abilities
- new restrictions
- new responsibilities
- new enemies
- new environments
- new social treatment
- new risks
- new opportunities
- new rules
- new knowledge
- new consequences
- new expectations

If the only difference is:

"the same thing but harder"

the level is too weak.

Redesign it around a qualitative change.


# 13. NO GENERIC FIVE-PHASE STORY

Do NOT automatically use:

FLOOR
→ FIRST YES
→ SWITCH
→ ADMINISTRATION
→ BILL / EXIT

Do NOT force every topic into:

weakness → success → promotion → burnout → successor.

The story architecture must come from the topic.

Possible structures include:

- discovery → mastery → escalation → crisis → payoff
- weakness → adaptation → breakthrough → consequence → transformation
- ignorance → discovery → obsession → revelation → aftermath
- outsider → insider → responsibility → crisis → changed perspective
- survival → competence → escalation → breaking point → aftermath

These are examples only.

Choose the structure that best fits the subject.


# 14. STORY ENGINE

Before writing the beats, identify the STORY ENGINE.

The story engine is the thing that naturally creates events.

Examples:

A power system:
new abilities create new problems.

A military hierarchy:
higher rank creates greater responsibility.

A dangerous world:
greater experience exposes the protagonist to greater threats.

A character's life:
each stage changes relationships and circumstances.

A technological progression:
each improvement creates new capabilities and new limitations.

A historical progression:
each event creates conditions for the next.

Do not invent an unrelated story engine.


# 15. SCENE-FIRST STORYTELLING

Beats are scenes or meaningful moments, not encyclopedia entries.

Every beat should show something happening.

Prefer:

EVENT → INFORMATION

over:

INFORMATION → INFORMATION

Example:

BAD:

"Rank 2 members are trusted with stronger enemies."

BETTER:

"At Rank 2, the escort leaves you alone with the target. Nobody checks whether you can handle it."

The second version teaches the same idea through an event.


# 16. EVERY BEAT MUST CHANGE SOMETHING

Each beat should contain at least one meaningful change.

Possible changes:

- protagonist learns something
- protagonist gains something
- protagonist loses something
- situation becomes harder
- situation becomes easier
- new person appears
- relationship changes
- rule is revealed
- ability is demonstrated
- threat changes
- goal changes
- environment changes
- expectation is overturned
- protagonist makes a decision

If nothing changes, the beat is probably filler.


# 17. INFORMATION DENSITY

Prefer beats that accomplish two or more functions.

A beat can:

- advance the story
- reveal a fact
- show the world
- develop a relationship
- demonstrate a power
- increase stakes
- create curiosity
- create a visual
- foreshadow something
- establish a consequence

Do not force every beat to contain many facts.

Clarity and entertainment are more important than density.


# 18. NO DEAD BEATS

Ask:

"If I delete this beat, does anything important change?"

If no:

rewrite it or remove it.

Never stretch the story simply to reach BEATS.

Never repeat an explanation just because another level needs a beat.


# 19. ESCALATION

Escalation does not simply mean bigger numbers.

It can mean:

- stronger opponents
- more dangerous environments
- less room for mistakes
- greater responsibility
- more complicated rules
- greater consequences
- harder decisions
- greater public attention
- more people depending on the protagonist
- loss of an old advantage
- increasing uncertainty
- increasingly strange situations

The progression should become more significant, not merely larger.


# 20. SUCCESS MUST CHANGE SOMETHING

Whenever the protagonist succeeds, ask:

"What does this success unlock?"

It may unlock:

- a new rank
- a new ability
- a new location
- a new responsibility
- a new enemy
- a new relationship
- new information
- a new problem

Success should move the story.


# 21. FAILURE MUST CHANGE SOMETHING

Whenever the protagonist fails, ask:

"What does this failure cause?"

A failure can:

- reveal a weakness
- change the strategy
- introduce a new threat
- damage a relationship
- force adaptation
- expose a hidden rule
- change the protagonist's goal

Do not insert failures simply because stories are supposed to have failures.


# 22. CONSEQUENCES

Actions should have consequences.

Consequences can be:

- physical
- social
- strategic
- emotional
- financial
- political
- relational
- environmental
- practical
- informational

Do NOT force arbitrary sacrifices.

Never add:

- random injuries
- random deaths
- random poverty
- random betrayal
- random romance
- random money problems

unless the topic naturally supports them.


# 23. CHARACTER AGENCY

The protagonist must actively participate.

Use actions such as:

- enters
- chooses
- refuses
- investigates
- fights
- escapes
- tests
- learns
- adapts
- challenges
- accepts
- protects
- confronts
- risks
- discovers
- changes

Do not make the protagonist spend the entire video watching other people do things.


# 24. WORLD CREATES THE EVENTS

This is one of the most important rules.

THE WORLD MUST CREATE THE EVENTS.

If the WORLD has:

- monsters, use the monsters
- ranks, use the ranks
- magic, use the magic
- politics, use the politics
- technology, use the technology
- war, use the war
- survival mechanics, use the survival mechanics
- social rules, use the social rules

The events should be difficult to transplant into another topic.

If the narration could be rewritten by changing only the nouns, it is too generic.


# 25. NO GENERIC STORY TEST

Temporarily replace all topic-specific nouns with generic nouns.

If the story still works as:

"you start weak, become stronger, gain respect, face a crisis, and reach the top"

then rewrite it.

The TOPIC must fundamentally determine what happens.


# 26. TOPIC-SPECIFIC NOVELTY

Prioritize interesting information that casual viewers may not know.

Look for:

- lesser-known mechanics
- surprising rules
- unusual consequences
- misunderstood details
- important exceptions
- strange historical facts
- hidden relationships
- unexpected limitations
- facts that change how the viewer understands the progression

Do not fill beats with universally obvious facts.


# 27. FACTS THROUGH EVENTS

Whenever possible, explain facts by showing them.

Do not write several encyclopedia-style beats.

Instead of:

"This rank allows X."

show:

"At this rank, you are finally trusted with X."

Then explain why that matters through what happens.

The audience should learn while watching the progression.


# 28. CHARACTER CAMEOS

Use named characters only when they affect the story.

A character can:

- teach something
- create a challenge
- create an opportunity
- reveal information
- demonstrate a level
- change a decision
- create a consequence
- represent what the protagonist is progressing toward

Never insert famous characters merely for recognition.


# 29. RELATIONSHIPS

Relationships should change the story.

Do not create artificial friendships, rivalries, romances, betrayals, or mentorships merely to add emotion.

If a relationship matters, show how it affects:

- decisions
- opportunities
- danger
- knowledge
- progression
- consequences


# 30. REVERSALS

Use meaningful reversals when appropriate.

Examples:

- success creates a new problem
- an ability has an unexpected weakness
- an advantage becomes a liability
- the next level is not what the protagonist expected
- a seemingly minor rule becomes important
- the protagonist discovers they misunderstood the system
- reaching the top creates a completely different problem

For approximately 20 beats, aim for roughly 2–4 meaningful turns when the topic naturally supports them.

Do not force reversals.


# 31. MIDPOINT

The middle should not simply repeat the opening.

Around the midpoint, introduce a meaningful change such as:

- new understanding
- new threat
- new ability
- new responsibility
- new environment
- reversal
- revelation
- change in objective

The viewer should feel that the story has entered a new phase.


# 32. PEAK

The highest or most important stage must feel different from the beginning.

Do not make the peak:

"the same thing with a bigger number."

The peak should demonstrate what the progression has been building toward.

It should reveal:

- what the protagonist can now do
- what they now face
- what the system ultimately demands
- what changes when someone reaches this stage


# 33. ENDING

The ending should feel like a payoff.

Do not automatically:

- add a successor
- add a moral
- explain the lesson
- say "and that is how..."
- repeat the entire progression

Choose the ending that naturally belongs to the topic.

Whenever possible, establish a small element near the beginning that can return at the end.

Possible elements:

- object
- location
- rule
- phrase
- enemy
- ability
- problem
- action
- visual composition


# 34. ENDING ECHO

Whenever it fits naturally, make the final beat echo the beginning.

For example:

Beginning:
the protagonist faces a problem they cannot handle.

Ending:
the protagonist encounters the same type of problem, but their position has completely changed.

The final image should show the difference.

Do not explain the symbolism.

Let the event communicate it.


# 35. SUCCESSOR ENDING

A successor/newcomer ending is OPTIONAL.

Use it only when it naturally fits the topic.

It is especially useful when the progression represents:

- ranks
- careers
- generations
- organizations
- training
- recurring systems
- inherited positions

Do NOT force a successor into topics where the progression naturally ends elsewhere.


# 36. HOOK → PAYOFF

Before writing, establish:

HOOK:
What happens immediately?

CENTRAL QUESTION:
What does the viewer now want to know?

PROGRESSION:
What changes from stage to stage?

MAJOR TURN:
Where does the viewer's understanding change?

PEAK:
What is the most important stage or event?

PAYOFF:
What earlier setup gains new meaning?

ENDING IMAGE:
What should the viewer see at the end?


# 37. NARRATION STYLE

Narration must be:

- second person
- present tense
- clear
- conversational
- fast-moving
- visual
- specific
- restrained
- natural
- easy to understand

Use "You" because the viewer is experiencing the progression.

Do not refer to the protagonist as:

- "the reference character"
- "the protagonist"
- "the viewer"

inside narration.

The narration should speak directly to the viewer as "You."


# 38. NARRATION RHYTHM

Avoid robotic structures.

BAD:

"You enter.

You fight.

You win.

You leave."

Use natural variation.

Example:

"You enter expecting one opponent. Instead, the room is already full, and nobody looks surprised to see you."

Vary:

- sentence length
- openings
- pacing
- sentence structure

Do not start every beat with "You."


# 39. NARRATION WORD COUNT

Each beat should contain:

14–19 words.

Aim for approximately 16–17 words.

Never add meaningless filler to reach the count.

Never destroy a strong sentence simply to hit the count.

If a sentence is slightly outside the range, rewrite it naturally.


# 40. BEAT STRUCTURE

A beat should generally contain:

ACTION + CHANGE

or:

SITUATION + CONSEQUENCE

or:

DISCOVERY + IMPLICATION

or:

DECISION + RESULT

or:

EVENT + NEW QUESTION

Do not use the same structure repeatedly.


# 41. TRANSITIONS

Each beat should naturally cause or motivate the next.

Think:

EVENT
→ CONSEQUENCE
→ NEW SITUATION
→ DECISION
→ RESULT
→ NEXT STAGE

Do not make every transition explicit.

Do not repeatedly write:

"Because of this..."

"Therefore..."

"The next step is..."


# 42. EMOTION

Emotion should come from the situation.

Do not constantly state:

"You feel scared."

"You feel sad."

"You feel proud."

Instead, show the situation producing the emotion.

The narration can remain restrained while the events carry emotional weight.


# 43. HUMOR

Humor is allowed when it naturally fits.

Possible forms:

- dry observation
- irony
- absurdity
- understatement
- character reaction
- unexpected consequence

Do not force jokes.


# 44. APHORISMS

Do NOT require an aphorism at the end of every level.

Do not manufacture fortune-cookie lines.

A memorable observation is allowed when earned by the story.

The event is more important than the quote.


# 45. NUMBERS

Use numbers only when they matter.

Good uses:

- actual ranks
- ages
- dates
- distances
- quantities
- durations
- statistics
- costs
- measurements
- power values
- historical figures

Do not invent numbers simply to make narration sound specific.

Specific events are usually better than meaningless statistics.


# 46. NO FAKE SPECIFICITY

Do not add random:

- salaries
- dates
- durations
- distances
- weather
- objects
- injuries
- costs
- body problems

unless they matter to the story or are factually relevant.

Specificity should make the story more memorable or informative.


# 47. IMAGE PROMPT NAMING LAW

Narration:

"You"

Image prompts:

"the reference character"

Never use "you" in image prompts to refer to the protagonist.

Never use the protagonist's personal name in image prompts unless explicitly required by the input.

Never call the protagonist:

- the narrator
- the protagonist
- the viewer

in image prompts.

Always use:

"the reference character"

This rule covers the protagonist ONLY. Every other character, creature, place
and organisation keeps its official name in image_prompt. See Section 51A.


# 48. BACK TO CAMERA

The reference character faces the camera by default. Vary his framing like any
other subject: front, three-quarter, profile, over the shoulder, back.

The reference art is a single front-facing pose. So ANY prompt using "from
behind", "over the shoulder", "rear view", "walking away" or "facing away" MUST
also contain, near those words, the BACK_CLAUSE string from the header.

Without it the model pastes the front-facing head onto a back-facing body and
the head comes out rotated.

Do not add the BACK_CLAUSE to a prompt that is not a back view.

Never describe his face, hair, skin, clothes, expression, mood, age or build in
any prompt, at any angle. The attached art already defines those, and
re-describing them competes with it. Only ever write what his BODY is DOING.


# 49. REFERENCE CHARACTER CONSISTENCY

The reference character must remain visually consistent.

Do not randomly change:

- clothing
- body proportions
- accessories
- hairstyle
- identity
- age

unless the topic explicitly requires a change.

The reference character does not age unless explicitly required.

Never describe their appearance at all. The attached reference art defines it.


# 50. IMAGE PROMPT PURPOSE

Every image must depict the actual event of the beat.

Do not generate generic:

- portraits
- standing poses
- empty rooms
- generic action scenes

unless they are actually what the beat is about.

The image should communicate the narration visually.


# 51. IMAGE PROMPT CONTENT

When relevant, specify:

- what the reference character is doing
- environment
- location
- important objects
- other characters
- background activity
- composition
- camera angle
- framing
- lighting
- atmosphere
- visual consequences
- continuity with previous images
- the BACK_CLAUSE, on back-view shots only

Prioritize concrete visual information over decorative adjectives.


# 51A. OFFICIAL NAMES IN IMAGE PROMPTS

This section covers everything in frame EXCEPT the protagonist. He is always
"the reference character" and is never described. Sections 47 and 49 still win
for him.

image_prompt uses the OFFICIAL name.

If the topic sits inside an existing universe, name things the way the source
names them: Gojo Satoru, Eren Yeager, a Grade 1 curse, the Survey Corps
uniform, Shiganshina. Never a generic stand-in when a real name exists.

AN OFFICIAL NAME IS NEVER ENOUGH ON ITS OWN.

The generator has never read the TOPIC and has no memory between beats. A name
it cannot picture becomes a blank, and it fills every blank from the real
world. So every named element must ALSO carry a full physical description in
the SAME prompt, every time it appears.

    WRONG: "Gojo stands in the doorway"
    RIGHT: "Gojo Satoru, a tall slim man with short spiked white hair and a
            black cloth band covering his eyes, in a dark navy high-collared
            uniform jacket, dark trousers and black boots, stands in the
            doorway"

Describe a character by: approximate height, build, hair colour and shape,
clothing silhouette, clothing colours and materials, boots, belts, accessories,
weapons, distinctive visible traits.

Describe a creature by: overall size, body proportions, anatomy, head shape,
limbs, surface colour and texture, distinctive structures.

Describe an object or weapon by: shape, size, material, colour, construction,
distinctive components, position in the scene.

Describe a location by: architecture, materials, colours, structures,
distinctive environmental objects.

An organisation is not visible. Naming a corps, a school, a police force or a
clan shows nothing. What is visible is the uniform, the equipment and the shape
of the insignia. The name tells YOU which thing to describe. The description is
the only part that reaches the picture.

Repeat the SAME core description for a recurring character every time they
appear: same build, same hair, same clothing silhouette and colours, same
accessories, same weapon. Story changes may be added on top. The core identity
never drifts.

Match the SOURCE's version, never the closest real-world equivalent. If you are
not certain how something looks in the source, look it up. A wrong uniform is
more obvious to a fan than a wrong date, and it sits in every frame it appears
in.


# 52. VISUAL PROGRESSION

Images should visually show progression.

Across the video, change things such as:

- environments
- scale
- danger
- opponents
- responsibilities
- abilities
- social situations
- objects
- lighting
- composition

Do not create 20 visually identical scenes.


# 53. LOCATION DESIGN

LOCATIONS: auto.

Determine locations from the story.

Do not force a fixed number of locations.

A location can persist across multiple beats if that is natural.

A location should change when the story requires a new environment.

Maintain continuity when it does not.


# 54. FACTUAL ACCURACY

For real-world topics:

Verify factual claims.

For fictional topics:

Verify canon claims whenever possible.

Do not:

- invent official rules
- invent official ranks
- invent powers
- invent relationships
- invent historical events
- invent statistics
- contradict established canon

If the story includes fictionalized "you experience this" events, they may be invented when necessary, but they must remain consistent with the source material.


# 55. INVENTED EVENTS

A progression story may require invented events involving the viewer.

Invented events are acceptable when:

- they do not contradict canon
- they do not falsely claim to be established events
- they make the progression understandable
- they fit the world's rules

Track intentionally invented details internally in facts_to_check.


# 56. RESEARCH

Research only what is necessary.

Prioritize:

- official sources
- primary sources
- reliable references
- high-quality reporting
- established canon sources

Do not turn the narration into a research report.

Research supports the story.


# 57. FACTS_TO_CHECK

Create a facts_to_check array.

For each important claim, track:

- claim
- type
- status
- source_needed
- invented

Possible types:

- canon
- real_world
- statistic
- date
- rank
- rule
- character
- location
- invented_event

Never present an uncertain factual claim as established truth.


# 58. STORY VS FACTS

Facts should serve the story.

Do not write:

"According to..."

"Research shows..."

"Sources say..."

inside narration unless specifically required by the format.

Instead, integrate facts into events.


# 59. FIRST FIVE BEATS

The first five beats are especially important.

They should generally accomplish:

1. Establish the first level/rank/stage and create the hook.
2. Make the situation more interesting.
3. Reveal an important part of what this level actually means.
4. Show the protagonist acting within that system.
5. Create the first meaningful turn, consequence, or reason to progress.

Do not spend the first five beats merely defining the system.


# 60. RETENTION RHYTHM

Do not let several consecutive beats do the same thing.

Whenever possible, introduce a:

- discovery
- consequence
- escalation
- decision
- reveal
- reversal
- new situation

every few beats.

Do not force a mathematical rhythm.

The purpose is to prevent the middle from becoming flat.


# 61. STORY MOMENTUM

Every beat should create forward movement.

Ask:

"Why does the viewer need the next beat?"

Possible answers:

- they want to know what happens
- they want to understand the new rule
- they want to see whether the protagonist succeeds
- they want to see the next rank
- they want to know the consequence
- they want to see the new ability
- they want to understand the reveal

If there is no reason to continue, strengthen the beat.


# 62. NO REPETITION

Do not repeat:

- the same information
- the same emotional reaction
- the same action
- the same type of image
- the same transition
- the same sentence structure
- the same explanation

unless repetition is intentionally used for payoff.


# 63. STORY PLAN

Before writing the script, internally determine:

- progression type
- actual progression stages
- level count
- opening rank/stage
- opening situation

- major events
- major discoveries
- major turns
- escalation
- peak
- ending payoff
- recurring elements

Do not skip planning.

Do not output hidden reasoning.


# 64. LEVEL COUNT

LEVELS: auto.

Determine the appropriate number of meaningful levels.

Do not arbitrarily force every topic into 4 or 5 levels.

If the progression has many stages, intelligently group related stages when necessary.

If the progression has few stages, use multiple meaningful beats within each stage.

Never skip a major stage simply because it is inconvenient.

When no official labels exist, create concise descriptive labels that accurately represent the progression.

# 69. IMAGE_PROMPT_ALT

image_prompt_alt is the RECOVERY prompt, not a caption. The pipeline writes it
to scenes_alt.txt line for line, and when the generator refuses the primary,
the runner retries that beat with the alternate. Retrying the same words gets
refused identically, so the alternate must differ in the two ways below.

It shows the SAME story moment: same main action, same characters, same
setting, same important objects. It must still make sense read against that
beat's narration.

DIFFERENCE ONE: EVERY OFFICIAL NAME IS GONE.

No character names, no creature names, no place names, no weapon names, no
organisation names, no franchise terminology. "the reference character" is not
an official name and stays: it is the pointer to the attached art. Section 47
still holds, and he is still never described.

Do NOT merely delete a name. REPLACE IT WITH DESCRIPTION. The alternate spends
words buying back everything the name was carrying for free.

    PRIMARY: "Gojo Satoru, a tall slim man with short spiked white hair and a
              black cloth band covering his eyes, in a dark navy high-collared
              uniform jacket, stands in the doorway"

    ALT:     "a tall slim young man with short spiked bright white hair and a
              wide black cloth band wrapped across his eyes, wearing a dark
              navy high-collared jacket with a zip front over a black shirt,
              dark trousers and tall black boots, stands in the doorway"

Expand a character by: height, proportions, build, skin tone, hair colour,
length and shape, face shape, clothing layers, colours, materials, boots,
belts, straps, accessories, weapons, posture.

Expand a creature by: proportions, size, anatomy, head shape, limbs, surface
colour, surface texture, distinctive structures.

Expand an object by: shape, size, materials, colours, construction, components,
position in the scene.

Expand a location by: building shape, architecture, materials, colours, doors,
windows, roofs, walls, streets, furniture.

THE ALTERNATE IS LONGER FOR THIS REASON ONLY.

The primary is the prompt that actually gets generated. The alternate runs only
on the minority of beats that get refused. So nothing may be held back from the
primary to make the alternate look richer. If the alternate mentions something
the primary does not, the PRIMARY is missing it and needs it back.

DIFFERENCE TWO: DIFFERENT STAGING.

Change at least one major visual variable and use a different framing family.
If the primary is a close up, the alternate is wide or medium, never another
close up with the words reordered. Variables: camera distance, foreground
object, character position, which character is closest to camera, visible
environmental feature.

SAFETY RECOVERY.

Move anything violent into its aftermath. A body becomes an empty coat. Drop
blood, wounds and bodies entirely.


# 70. FINAL STORY CHECK

Before outputting, ask:

HOOK:

- Does Beat 1 establish the first level/rank/stage?
- Does it also create curiosity?
- Is it an actual situation rather than a definition?
- Would it make someone continue watching?

PROGRESSION:

- Are the stages accurate?
- Is the progression easy to follow?
- Does every level feel different?
- Does each transition change the situation?

STORY:

- Is the protagonist active?
- Does something meaningful happen in every beat?
- Are there consequences?
- Are there discoveries?
- Are there meaningful turns?
- Is the middle still interesting?
- Does the peak feel different from the beginning?

TOPIC:

- Does the WORLD create the events?
- Are the events specific to the TOPIC?
- Would the story still work if the topic were replaced?
- If yes, rewrite it.

FACTS:

- Are important factual claims verified?
- Are invented events clearly tracked?
- Are there canon contradictions?
- Are numbers actually useful?

ENDING:

- Does the ending pay something off?
- Does it connect naturally to the progression?
- Does it avoid an unnecessary moral?
- Does it avoid a forced successor?
- Does it feel like the natural ending of THIS topic?

IMAGES:

- Does every image depict its beat?
- Is the reference character consistent?
- Does every back-view prompt carry the BACK_CLAUSE, and no other prompt?
- Are locations consistent?
- Does the visual progression match the narrative?
- Does every named character, creature and organisation in an image_prompt also
  carry a full physical description?
- Does every image_prompt_alt drop all official names, replace each with
  description, and change the staging?


# 71. FINAL RETENTION TEST

Read only the narration.

Imagine hearing it for the first time.

After every beat, ask:

"Would I want the next beat?"

If several beats in a row produce no curiosity, consequence, discovery, escalation, or payoff, rewrite them.

The script should not feel like:

"Here is Rank 1.
Here is Rank 2.
Here is Rank 3.
Here is Rank 4."

It should feel like:

"You are at Rank 1.
This is what happens.
This is what you discover.
This changes your situation.
You finally reach Rank 2.
Now the rules are different.
This creates a new problem.
You adapt.
You reach the next stage.
And eventually, you understand what the entire progression was leading toward."


# 72. FINAL UNIVERSALITY TEST

Before outputting, ask:

"Would this exact storytelling template work for a completely different topic?"

The answer should be YES.

The prompt must provide the storytelling framework while allowing the TOPIC and WORLD to determine:

- the events
- the progression
- the challenges
- the characters
- the consequences
- the locations
- the escalation
- the payoff

Never inject topic-specific assumptions that are not present in the input.

The template is universal.

The STORY is not.

Only output the final valid JSON.
