IMPORTANT LOCATION RULE:

The input script may contain narration only and may contain NO locations.

Do NOT assume that Pass 1 provides locations.

If Pass 1 contains no locations, you MUST creatively generate the locations
yourself from the narration before writing the visual prompts.

The generated locations must be physically believable, visually interesting,
specific to the subject being discussed, and useful for staging the
reference character.

Locations are NOT drawn. Every frame is a plain white page (Section 0B).
A location is only a name that groups beats into scenes: the video dissolves
where the location changes and hard cuts inside one. Pick locations to split
the video into its natural chapters.

Do not merely invent generic locations such as "room", "office", "studio",
"warehouse", "lab", "street", or "hallway" unless the narration genuinely
requires that exact environment.

Instead, infer what physical environment would make each part of the
narration interesting to see.

The location name may be a literal place where the event happened OR a
storytelling environment, named for what that chapter of the narration is
about. The name is never drawn.

The location does not need to be historically literal when a more useful
visual storytelling environment would communicate the narration better.

For example:

narration about lost NASA recordings
→ "forgotten aerospace archive"

narration about an absurd conspiracy
→ "cramped conspiracy evidence workshop"

narration about strange scientific experiments
→ "crowded experimental workshop"

narration about political decisions
→ "government back-room briefing chamber"

narration about old radio transmissions
→ "underground radio monitoring room"

narration about unexplained photographs
→ "forensic photography archive"

narration about conflicting historical evidence
→ "overcrowded historical evidence room"

The generated location should feel specific, memorable, and visually useful.

Do NOT make every location comedic.

When the narration is absurd, humorous, suspicious, or ridiculous, the physical
location may contain slightly absurd but believable visual staging.

The humour comes from the situation, the reference character's reaction, a
short label, or a rare photo cutout (Section 0B), never from scenery.

The location must still make sense for the subject.

The primary prompt uses canonical names for recognizable subjects OTHER THAN
THE REFERENCE CHARACTER.

The reference character is ALWAYS represented by the exact phrase:

reference character

The alternate removes canonical names for OTHER named subjects and replaces
them with physical descriptions, while the reference character remains:

reference character

0B. THE WHITE PAGE LOOK

This section overrides every later section about rooms, scenery, materials,
colour and text.

Every frame is a plain white page. Never describe a room, floor, wall, sky,
ground, horizon, furniture, buildings or scenery unless that one object IS the
point of the beat.

Each frame holds the reference character plus at most TWO other things.

A crowd or group is at most three doodle figures.

Other people, famous ones included, are simple doodle figures identified by
one or two signature features (long braids, a scarlet red racing suit). Never
ask for them to look realistic.

Colour only one or two key objects. Name the colour on the object:
one scarlet red racing car.

Name the exact kind of every vehicle and object, never only its brand or its
owner. A brand name alone draws that brand's best-known product, which can be
the wrong kind of thing entirely.

Simple and big beats detailed and small. If the frame needs explaining, it has
too much in it.

LABELS

At most 1 beat in 4 may carry words, and only when the words are the joke or
the key fact. 1 to 3 words, in double quotes, attached to an object:

a big sign reading "NOPE"
a price tag reading "$15 MILLION"
a speech bubble reading "WHY"

Never more than one label per beat. Labels go in image_prompt or in an edit
(Section 0C), never in narration.

Draw every label BIG: a big sign, a big tag, a big speech bubble. A small one
comes out too small to read in the video.

PHOTO CUTOUTS

Aim for about 1 beat in 10 (7 or 8 in a 75-beat script), spread across the
whole video, not bunched in one section. Hard limit: 1 beat in 6, never in
two beats in a row.

Use one when seeing the REAL object makes the beat land harder than a doodle
would: an absurd price, a ridiculous real thing, a "yes, this actually
exists" moment, or a real surviving artefact the narration points at. Never
just because an object is mentioned.

ONE thing only: one object, or one famous real person from plan.cast. Never
a face or body part on its own, an animal, a place, or the reference
character.

A real person as a photo cutout is the collage look: the photo of that one
person, pasted into a doodle world. Everything else in the frame stays doodle:
the reference character, the vehicles, the props, other people. Use it where
seeing the REAL person lands harder than a doodle would, usually the beat that
introduces them. Section 0D still holds: only doing something true of them.

Write it with this exact phrase:

a real photo cutout of <object or person>, roughly pasted onto the drawing

Example:

reference character stares, eyes wide, at a real photo cutout of a Formula 1
steering wheel, roughly pasted onto the drawing, medium shot

The image_prompt_alt of a photo beat replaces the cutout with a doodle of the
same object or person, without the name. The alt is the fallback when Flow
refuses, so it must be the safe version.

A photo person stays a photo through the edits after it. Keep those edits to
pose and gesture (folds his arms, waves); never ask an edit to change a photo
person's face.

0C. CHAINED FRAMES

This section overrides every later section about how many prompts a beat
gets, and about varying camera, placement and framing from beat to beat.

The video is animated by editing. Each location opens with one drawn picture.
Every later picture in that location is a small edit of the picture just
before it: the image tool sees only that previous picture and your edit line,
and changes nothing else. Small changes, cut together every 1.5 to 4 seconds,
are what make the stills move.

THE COMMENT QUESTION

Near the middle, Pass 1 has 2 short beats that ask the viewer to guess
something, and a later beat that gives the answer. They are ordinary beats of
their chapter: same location, chained edits like every other beat, no
location of their own. On the question beats, the thing being guessed is in
the picture but hidden: covered by a cloth, turned away, or replaced by one
large question mark, and the reference character looks at it curiously. On
the answer beat, show the answer clearly (the full trophy shelf, the number on
a banner). Never draw a comment box, phone, app screen, buttons or any other
interface.

PLAN EACH LOCATION AS ONE SCENE

Before writing any prompt for a location, decide:

what its opening picture shows
what its last picture shows
the steps from one to the other, one per picture, in the order the narration
gets to them

Every edit belongs on that path. Edits that go nowhere make a slideshow of the
same picture.

So a beat carries:

image_prompt and image_prompt_alt ONLY on the first beat of each location,
and on a beat that redraws (see REDRAW below). Every rule in this prompt about
image_prompt and image_prompt_alt applies to these. Wherever a later section
says "every beat" about them, read it as every beat that has them.

edits on every OTHER beat: exactly one edit line, a list holding one string.

ONE PICTURE PER BEAT

Every beat is exactly one picture: its image_prompt, or its one edit. Never
both, never more than one edit. The picture appears as the beat starts and
stays up for the whole beat, so the beat's length is how long it is on screen
(short beats about 1.5 seconds, long ones 3 to 4). The narration already
mixes short, medium and long beats, so the pictures get that rhythm for free.

Make each beat's edit show what THAT beat says, since it appears exactly as
the beat is spoken.

WRITING AN EDIT

One line of 4 to 15 words that says only what changes. Start with the thing
that changes:

the character's eyes go wide
the crocodile opens its jaws wide
a big sign reading "LATE" appears above the door
the measuring tape disappears

Change one or two things per edit, and make the change visible at a glance. A
cut between two nearly identical pictures looks like a glitch, not motion.

Good edits: a pose or expression change, a thing appearing or disappearing, a
thing moving across the page, a label appearing, a figure turning, a thing
changing colour or size.

SAY WHERE AND HOW BIG

Anything an edit adds gets a position relative to what is already in the
picture, and a size. Without them the image tool puts it wherever there is
room: "a car appears behind the crocodile" came back with the car sitting on
the crocodile's back, and "a small tag" came back too small to read.

one big red car appears on the ground at frame right, past the crocodile's tail
a big white tag reading "6 M" hangs from the end of the tape

USE THE WHOLE PAGE

The page has five spots: frame left, frame centre, frame right, top of the
frame, and the bottom foreground. Left alone, every new thing lands in the
same spot as the last one, so a whole location looks like one picture with
its right side swapped out.

- Every edit that adds something names its spot. No position is not allowed.
- A new thing never goes in the spot the previous added thing used, unless
  it replaces it on purpose.
- Within one location, use at least three different spots.
- Labels and tags go at the top of the frame or right next to what they
  label, not always above the same thing.
- The reference character does not have to stay in one place: on a redraw
  or a new location, put him at frame centre or frame right sometimes.
- Vary size too: one thing big in the foreground, the next small and far.

REACTIONS ARE THE SAFEST MOTION

When a line adds nothing new to the picture, change the character's FACE or
HEAD: his eyes go wide, his eyebrows rise, his eyelids droop, his mouth drops
open, he tilts his head, he leans in, a sweat drop appears. These are the most
reliable edits there are. Never let two pictures in a row look the same.

TWO ARMS, TWO HANDS

An edit that changes an arm pose often ADDS an arm instead of moving the old
one: "the character checking his wrist", while one hand held a bat up, came
back with three arms. "The character holding up one finger" was given to a
batter gripping his bat with both hands, who grew a third hand. So:

Keep track of what each figure's hands are doing through the chain, from the
opening picture on.

Never give the character an arm or hand action while either hand is
holding something. No pointing, waving, wrist checks, crossed arms, shrugs,
head scratches, clapping or holding up fingers. Use a face or head reaction
instead, or make the change around him (a sign, a tag, an object appearing).

When both hands are free, at most ONE arm action every 4 edits, and say which
arm and where it ends up: "the character's right arm points at the sign".
Never an arm action and a new held object in the same edit.

The same goes for every other figure in the picture: once a figure holds
something, its arms stay as they are.

In edits the reference character is "the character". Never write reference
character in an edit: an edit has no character art attached, only the
previous picture.

Refer to everything already in the picture with the words that introduced it
("the crocodile", never "the reptile"). Refer to other people by their visible
feature, never by name: an edit has no alternate to fall back on when a name
is refused.

Never ask an edit for a new camera: no close-up, wide shot, angle, zoom or
camera. An edit keeps the framing. A new framing is a redraw.

Never describe the drawing style, the page or the background in an edit. They
carry over by themselves.

THE PAGE STAYS SIMPLE

Section 0B's limits hold for every picture in the chain, not just the first:
the reference character plus at most two other things. Edits pile up, so an
edit that adds a thing when two are already on the page must remove one in
the same line:

the measuring tape disappears and one big red car appears on the ground at
frame right, past the crocodile's tail

Labels stay at most 1 beat in 4, counting labels added by edits. A label stays
on the page until an edit removes it, so the next beat's edit removes it,
unless that beat is still about it.

A photo cutout of an object is added by an edit, never by a redraw. The limits
in PHOTO CUTOUTS still apply.

REDRAW

Every edit drifts a little and the drift compounds: the page turns grey and
grainy and the lines go soft. After about 5 edits in a row, give the next
beat a fresh image_prompt and image_prompt_alt even when
the location has not changed, describing the scene as it now stands. Redraw
also when a beat needs a new framing.

Do not redraw more often than that. Every redraw is a visible jump.

The opening image_prompt of a location sets the stage for the edits after it:
place the reference character and the things the next beats will act on, with
room to move. Section 23's variety of placement and framing now happens at
redraws, not on every beat.

Example, three beats in one location:

{
"narration": "The saltwater crocodile is the largest reptile alive today.",
"location": "northern australian riverbank",
"image_prompt": "reference character stands at frame left looking curious at one huge green saltwater crocodile lying beside him, wide shot",
"image_prompt_alt": "reference character stands at frame left, curious, beside one huge green crocodile with a long bumpy back lying flat, wide shot"
},
{
"narration": "Big males can grow longer than six meters.",
"location": "northern australian riverbank",
"edits": [
"a long yellow measuring tape appears along the crocodile from nose to tail, a big white tag reading \"6 M\" at its end"
]
},
{
"narration": "That is longer than a small car.",
"location": "northern australian riverbank",
"edits": [
"the tape disappears and one big red car appears on the ground at frame right, past the crocodile's tail, clearly shorter than it"
]
}

1. THE REFERENCE CHARACTER: PERSISTENT VISUAL NARRATOR

This is the most important character rule.

The recurring main character is called:

reference character

The exact lowercase phrase reference character MUST appear in every
image_prompt.

The exact lowercase phrase reference character MUST appear in every
image_prompt_alt.

The reference character is a persistent visual narrator/observer.

They are allowed to appear in every beat even when the narration does not
explicitly mention them.

Their presence does NOT mean that they are one of the historical people being
discussed.

They are the visual stand-in for the viewer/narrator and can quietly react to
the information being presented.

The reference character should therefore usually be doing one of these:

sitting at the desk
leaning against the desk
standing beside a visual prop
looking at a diagram
staring at a photograph
watching another character
sitting on the couch
standing beside the shadow board
examining an object
looking skeptical
looking bored
looking annoyed
looking unimpressed
looking confused
looking curious
looking tired
looking surprised
looking toward the relevant evidence
quietly observing the event

The reference character should NOT automatically become the subject of the
historical event.

For example, if the narration discusses Apollo astronauts, do NOT turn the
reference character into an Apollo astronaut unless the script specifically
requires that.

Instead, the reference character can observe the astronaut material from the
edge of the frame.

2. REFERENCE CHARACTER EMOTIONAL BASELINE

The reference character has a recurring personality:

slightly bored, skeptical, unimpressed, or mildly irritated by the absurdity
of what is being discussed.

This personality should appear subtly throughout the visuals.

The reference character may look:

bored
tired
skeptical
mildly annoyed
unimpressed
deadpan
confused
curious
resigned

Choose the emotion that best fits the beat.

Do not make the character wildly expressive in every shot.

The default should be a restrained, slightly bored or unimpressed reaction.

When the narration contains a particularly absurd claim, the reference
character may visibly look more annoyed or incredulous.

When the narration reveals useful evidence, the reference character may look
curious or interested.

When the narration reaches a major reveal, the reference character may look
genuinely annoyed, disappointed, surprised, or skeptical as appropriate.

When the narration reaches the final payoff, the reference character may look
tired, resigned, or amused.

IMPORTANT:

Narration emotion tags such as:

[sarcastic]
[laughs]
[annoyed]
[thoughtful]

describe the AUDIO DELIVERY.

They do NOT automatically require the reference character to perform the same
emotion physically.

Use the narration's meaning and context to choose the reference character's
visual reaction.

3. THE REFERENCE CHARACTER MUST NOT HIJACK THE SCENE

The reference character is persistent, but they must remain visually secondary
when the beat describes an important historical event.

The reference character can be:

foreground observer
side-positioned observer
seated nearby
standing at the edge
partially turned toward the evidence
looking at a diagram
watching another character

But do not make the reference character replace:

astronauts
NASA engineers
Soviet operators
historical figures
crowds
workers
scientists
other required subjects

The reference character is an observer unless the narration specifically
requires them to participate.

WHEN THE BEAT IS A STORY EVENT, THE STORY OWNS THE FRAME

A beat describing something HAPPENING is staged as that thing happening, with
the named characters in the foreground performing it, and the reference
character pushed to the edge: at the frame edge, seen from behind, small in
the background, or partly out of frame.

Within any run of beats that tells one story, AT LEAST HALF the beats must be
staged this way.

The failure to avoid is a whole video of one man standing beside an object
with an expression on his face. It is the same picture sixty times, the
objects become interchangeable, and the story never appears on screen at all.

Count it before you finish. If most beats in a section are the reference
character plus a prop, the section is wrong and must be restaged.

The reference character still appears in every beat. He just stops being the
subject of most of them.

4. REFERENCE CHARACTER IS ALWAYS PRESENT

For this pipeline, the reference character is a persistent recurring visual
element.

Therefore:

EVERY image_prompt MUST contain:

reference character

EVERY image_prompt_alt MUST contain:

reference character

The reference character must also be visually present in every resulting frame.

If the beat contains a historical scene, place the reference character as a
visually separate observer.

If the beat contains an object, diagram, photograph, or physical demonstration,
place the reference character beside it or observing it.

If the beat discusses something happening elsewhere, the reference character
can react to a physical representation of that subject.

Examples:

reference character sits beside a lunar photograph

reference character stands beside a shadow diagram

reference character watches two aerospace workers examine equipment

reference character sits on the couch with a skeptical expression while a
large lunar photograph rests on the nearby table

Do NOT omit the reference character simply because the narration does not
explicitly mention them.

5. THE REFERENCE CHARACTER IS NOT THE ONLY CHARACTER

This remains important.

reference character identifies the recurring visual narrator.

It does NOT mean that every person in the frame must be the reference
character.

If the script requires another person or group to be visually represented,
show that person or group as a separate subject.

Never replace them with reference character.

Never merge them into reference character.

The reference character may observe them.

6. CHARACTER CASTING RULE

For every beat, determine the complete visual cast.

Internally identify:

reference character
other named characters
other unnamed characters
groups or crowds

Then determine which subjects are visually necessary.

The reference character is always required.

Other characters are only required when the narration, plan, or visual event
actually benefits from showing them.

IMPORTANT:

A person merely mentioned in narration does NOT automatically need to appear.

For example:

"Shot in a Hollywood basement by a famous movie director."

does not require showing a specific movie director.

The visual can instead show:

reference character + film equipment + staged lunar set

unless a specific director is genuinely being depicted.

7. CANONICAL NAMES IN PRIMARY PROMPTS

For characters OTHER THAN the reference character:

If an official or canonical name exists and that character is visually present,
the primary prompt MUST use the canonical name.

This applies to real people AND to fictional characters, creatures and objects
from a named show, film, book, game or comic.

Real people:

Neil Armstrong
Buzz Aldrin
Stanley Kubrick
Nikita Khrushchev

Fictional subjects, named together with the work so the name is unambiguous:

Tanjiro Kamado from Demon Slayer
Nezuko from Demon Slayer
Alphonse Elric from Fullmetal Alchemist
Gojo Satoru from Jujutsu Kaisen
a Titan from Attack on Titan

Write the name the way the audience would say it out loud.

DO NOT STRIP THE NAME OUT

When the narration is about a named work, replacing its characters with
generic strangers is the most damaging thing this pass can do.

"a lean teenage boy in a dark navy school blazer" is not Light Yagami. It is a
stranger. The viewer came to see Light Yagami, the narration says Light
Yagami, and the picture shows somebody else. Every beat of that section is
then carried by the voice alone and the image contributes nothing.

The primary prompt names whoever the narration names and shows on screen.

Do not pre-emptively remove a name because the character might be
copyrighted, trademarked or recognizable. That decision belongs to the image
tool and to the alternate prompt, which exists precisely so a refused name has
a fallback. It is not made in this pass.

A NAME ALONE IS NOT ENOUGH

The name tells the image model WHO. It does not guarantee the model knows
them, and a name it does not recognize renders as a generic person.

So every canonical name in a primary is followed by that character's ONE
signature visible feature: the single detail that identifies them in
silhouette.

Nezuko from Demon Slayer, a bamboo muzzle strapped across her mouth
Alphonse Elric, a towering hollow steel suit of armor
Gojo Satoru, a white-haired man in a black cloth blindfold
Yuji Itadori, a pink-haired boy in a black school uniform
a Titan, an enormous bald giant with an oversized grinning mouth

Choose each character's signature feature ONCE and reuse it WORD FOR WORD in
every beat that character appears in.

That feature is also what makes the alternate a real backup: the alternate is
the same sentence with the name deleted and the signature feature kept, so a
refused name still produces a recognizable picture.

NAME THE WORK TOO

When a beat is about the show itself rather than about a person, name the work
and attach it to a visible object from it.

Do not invent a canonical name.

Do not use a canonical name merely because the narration mentions a person if
that person is not visually present.

Do not use a canonical name for the reference character. See 8.

8. REFERENCE CHARACTER CANONICAL NAME OVERRIDE

If the narration names the person represented by the reference character,
DO NOT use that person's name in either image prompt.

Always use:

reference character

The reference character's identity is controlled by the external reference
image/system and must never be replaced with a canonical name.

9. OTHER NAMED CHARACTERS IN ALTERNATE PROMPTS

For characters OTHER THAN the reference character:

PRIMARY:

canonical name + physical scene

ALT:

physical description + physical scene

The alternate must describe the same person.

Do not replace a named historical person with reference character.

ALTERNATE LENGTH

A canonical name costs two words. A description that actually identifies the
person costs about twenty. The alternate therefore does NOT share the primary's
budget.

Base target 20 to 30 words, as in the primary.
Add up to 20 further words for EACH named character whose name you replaced.

no named characters   20 to 30 words
one named character   up to 50 words
two named characters  up to 70 words

Spend the extra words on the characters, not on the scene or the location.

Describe each named character with:

build and height
hair colour and shape
face covering or eyewear
one garment with its colour
the signature feature chosen for them in 7, word for word

The alternate is the primary with the canonical name deleted and the signature
feature kept. Nothing else about the character changes.

Write each character's description ONCE and reuse it WORD FOR WORD in every
beat that character appears in. A description paraphrased between beats is a
different instruction to the image model each time, and the character visibly
drifts across the video.

10. MULTIPLE CHARACTERS

If the script requires multiple characters, all required characters must appear
in both primary and alternate.

The reference character must also remain present.

Example:

PRIMARY:

reference character watches Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin beside the lunar
module, Armstrong on the left, Aldrin beside an equipment rack, medium
three-shot

ALT:

reference character watches two men in bulky white pressure suits with clear
bubble helmets and rectangular life-support packs beside the lunar module, the
taller one on the left, the shorter one beside an equipment rack, wider
three-shot

The alternate must preserve:

same number of required characters
same character identities
same relative relationships
same action
same story moment

The reference character remains present in both.

11. GROUPS AND CROWDS

If the narration requires a group, preserve the group in both prompts.

The reference character remains separate from the group unless the narration
specifically makes them a member of it.

Example:

PRIMARY:

reference character watches three NASA engineers in headsets crowded around
one blue console, all turning toward him, medium-wide shot

ALT:

reference character watches three doodle engineers in pale shirts and headsets
crowded around one blue console, all turning toward him, wider side angle

12. DO NOT INVENT CHARACTERS

The reference character is NOT considered an invented character.

They are a mandatory persistent visual narrator.

Additional characters should only appear when:

the narration explicitly requires them
the visual event logically requires them
the plan requires them
they are needed to depict the described interaction

Do not add random:

astronauts
engineers
soldiers
scientists
bystanders
crowds
family members
movie directors

just to make the frame more populated.

The only exception is the mandatory reference character.

13. SAME CAST IN PRIMARY AND ALTERNATE

The primary and alternate must depict the same required cast.

The reference character must appear in both.

If PRIMARY contains:

reference character + Neil Armstrong + Buzz Aldrin

ALT must contain:

reference character + physical Neil Armstrong description +
physical Buzz Aldrin description

Do not remove a required character in the alternate.

Do not introduce a new required character in the alternate.

Do not merge two distinct characters into one.

Do not replace any required character with the reference character.

14. SCRIPT IS READ-ONLY

Every narration string in the output must be byte-identical to the input.

This includes emotion tags.

Never:

reword
resize
split
merge
fix grammar
swap words
add clauses
remove clauses
reorder beats
add beats
drop beats

If a location already exists in Pass 1, preserve it unchanged.

If a beat has no location, generate one.

Generated locations are the ONLY new narrative-supporting information that may
be introduced by the visual pass.

The visual prompt may interpret the narration, but it must NEVER modify the
narration.

15. LOCATION GENERATION

The input may contain no locations.

Before creating image prompts, inspect every beat and determine whether it has
a usable physical location.

If a usable location is present:

preserve it exactly
reuse it consistently
set its visual_anchor to ""

If a location is absent:

invent a concrete physical location based on the narration.

If the existing location is too generic to support useful visualization, you
may replace it with a more specific generated physical location ONLY when the
location was not explicitly supplied by the original plan.

Never overwrite an explicit Pass 1 location merely because you prefer another
one.

Every beat must ultimately have a physical location.

A generated location should answer:

"Where could I physically stage this narration so that the resulting image is
interesting and immediately connected to the subject?"

THE REAL SETTING WINS WHEN THERE IS ONE

If the narration retells a story that has its own known setting, use that
setting. A metaphor location, an evidence room or a storytelling environment
is for narration that has NO physical place of its own.

Getting this wrong looks like this: a section about a modern Japanese school
story staged in a wood-panelled detective study with a chessboard, a brass
magnifying lens and a deerstalker hat. Those props come from a different
story. They tell the viewer they are watching the wrong thing.

Do not import the furniture of a genre the subject does not belong to.

Match the location to the subject's own world: its country, its period, its
kind of building.

The location may be:

literal
reconstructed
archival
investigative
scientific
historical
technical
domestic
institutional
industrial
governmental
eccentric
slightly absurd

depending on the narration.

Do not use random locations unrelated to the subject.

16. LOCATION DESIGN

Locations are chapter names, not pictures. Keep them short and concrete so a
reader of the JSON can see where each chapter starts:

the pit lane
the factory
race weekend
the money

Aim for 4 to 8 locations in a video. A location change is a dissolve, so do
not change location every few beats.
If the script says "format": "facts_short" (a ~50 second facts reel), use one
location per fact instead: 3 to 5 in total, and no "the comment break".

17. LOCATION VISUAL ANCHOR

plan.locations[].visual_anchor is ALWAYS "".

The white page is the only background. Anything written here would be pasted
into every beat of the location and draw a room over the white page.

18. LOCATION CONTINUITY

If multiple beats clearly belong to the same conceptual environment, reuse the
same location.

Do not invent a completely new room for every sentence.

A location can evolve through:

movable objects
characters
evidence
equipment
temporary activity

In this video a location is one continuously animated scene: it opens with one
picture and evolves through edits (Section 0C). The camera only changes at a
redraw.

Create a new location when:

the subject changes substantially
the physical environment needs to change
the story moves to a fundamentally different setting
another environment provides substantially better visualization

Maintain strong continuity within recurring locations.

19. LOCATION NAME RULE

Every beat MUST have a location.

Every location must have a short concrete name.

Good:

"forgotten aerospace archive"

"underground radio monitoring room"

"cramped evidence workshop"

"government map room"

Bad:

"the mystery"

"the conspiracy"

"the problem"

"the evidence"

"the past"

A location name must describe a physical place.

20. DO NOT WRITE THE LOCATION INTO THE BEAT

The location is never drawn. Do not describe it, its walls, its floor or its
furniture in a beat.

The beat prompt describes what changes from beat to beat:

characters
actions
body positions
reference character reaction
important objects
camera position
framing
story-specific changes

21. SETTING ANCHOR

plan.setting_anchor is ALWAYS "". Same reason as Section 17.

22. PRIMARY IMAGE PROMPT

Each image_prompt is one single line.

Target 12 to 20 words with no named characters in the frame.

Add up to 10 further words for EACH named character, to pay for their
canonical name and their signature visible feature:

no named characters    12 to 20 words, hard ceiling 24
one named character    up to 30 words
two named characters   up to 40 words

Spend the extra words on the characters, never on scenery. There is no
scenery: the frame is a white page.

A name is never dropped to stay inside a word count. If the prompt is too
long, cut scenery, cut the second object, cut the adjective on the furniture.
Never cut the name.

Start lowercase.

Do not include the location name.

The primary prompt must combine:

reference character
canonical names for other named characters when visually present
each named character's signature visible feature
physical action or pose
at most two important objects or figures
the colour of one or two key objects
framing
a clear, exaggerated reaction from the reference character

The reference character does not have to be the main subject.

Example:

reference character stands with a bored skeptical expression beside one
astronaut doodle planting a red and white flag, medium-wide shot

23. REFERENCE CHARACTER PLACEMENT

Placement is chosen only where a picture is drawn fresh: a location's first
beat and a redraw (Section 0C). Inside a chain he stays where he is unless an
edit moves him.

Do not force the reference character into the center of every frame.

Vary their placement naturally:

foreground left
foreground right
seated in the background
standing beside the evidence
leaning against furniture
partially turned toward the subject
beside a board
beside a photograph
seated on the couch
standing near the edge of the frame

The reference character should feel like a recurring host silently reacting to
the explanation.

24. REFERENCE CHARACTER REACTION VARIATION

Avoid repeating the exact same reaction in every beat.

Inside a chain a reaction changes through an edit: "the character raises one
eyebrow", "the character's eyes go wide".

Vary subtle reactions according to context:

bored
skeptical
unimpressed
curious
mildly annoyed
confused
resigned
surprised
thoughtful
amused
disappointed

THE DEFAULT REACTION FOLLOWS THE SCRIPT'S STANCE

There is no single default mood. Read the narration and the plan and decide
what the script is doing, then pick the resting state from that:

the script argues AGAINST its subject (a debunk, a myth, a bad idea)
  resting state: skeptical, unimpressed, mildly irritated

the script argues FOR its subject (a recommendation, a list of good things,
praise, a pitch)
  resting state: interested, warming, increasingly won over

the script is neutral explanation
  resting state: attentive, mildly curious

A recommendation video whose host looks bored beside every recommendation is
arguing with its own narration. The picture always loses that argument,
because the viewer believes the face.

THE REACTION MUST AGREE WITH THE LINE IT SITS UNDER

Never pair a praising, awed, excited or emotional narration line with a bored,
unimpressed, deadpan or reluctant reaction.

Never pair a mocking or deflating line with an admiring reaction.

Across a whole video the reactions should travel. Doubtful at the top, moved
or convinced by the end, if that is the journey the narration takes.

Default toward restrained reactions.

Do not make every beat exaggerated.

Do not make the character smile in every frame.

Do not make the character angry in every frame.

Do not make the character point at the camera in every frame.

25. PHYSICAL DESCRIPTION

Every non-named person must be physically described.

Every group must be physically described.

Every creature must be physically described.

Every vehicle must be physically described.

Every newly introduced major object must be physically described.

The reference character does not need to be physically re-described if the
reference system already provides their appearance.

BAD:

two engineers stand nearby

GOOD:

two engineers in pale blue shirts, dark trousers, black shoes, and headsets

29. OBJECT PLACEMENT

Place things relative to the reference character or the frame, never relative
to walls, floors or ceilings, which do not exist:

beside the reference character
at frame left
held in both hands
towering over the reference character
crossing the foreground

BAD:

a steel cabinet set against the back wall

GOOD:

one grey steel cabinet towering over the reference character

30. ONE OBJECT OF EACH TYPE

Do not unnecessarily multiply identical objects.

When exactly one object matters, use:

one
a single

This reduces unwanted object multiplication.

NEVER ASK FOR A COUNT ABOVE FIVE

The image model cannot count. Past about five it produces an approximate
smear, so an exact number in a prompt is spent words that buy an unreadable
frame.

BANNED in any prompt:

thirty-seven slim black video cases
sixty-four episodes on a shelf
ten small gold trophy cups
rows of twelve desks

Numerals and spelled-out numbers above five are both banned.

Show quantity by CONTRAST instead, which survives any render:

one black notebook beside one enormous heap of identical notebooks
a single red case standing in front of a wall of black cases
one short shelf beside one shelf running the whole length of the wall

Up to five of something is fine, and only when the count itself matters.

31. POSITIVE CONSTRUCTION

Describe visible things.

Avoid unnecessary negative constructions.

BAD:

an empty room with no windows

GOOD:

33. FRAMING

34. BACK-OF-CAMERA CONTINUITY

If using:

from behind
rear view
walking away
facing away
over the shoulder

make the physical orientation explicit.

Do not describe front-facing anatomy in a rear-facing shot.

WHEN THE REFERENCE CHARACTER IS SEEN FROM BEHIND, SAY HE HAS NO FACE

The reference image is a single front-facing pose. Asked for his back, the
image model has nothing to work from, pastes the front-facing head onto a
rear-facing body, and the head comes out rotated 180 degrees.

Any prompt that turns the reference character away from the camera MUST carry
this clause, word for word:

showing the back of his plain white head with no face

This applies to the alternate as well as the primary.

It applies only when the REFERENCE CHARACTER is the one turned away. If the
camera sits behind another character's shoulder, phrase it as "past the
shoulder of X" so the rule is not triggered for the wrong person.

39B. DO NOT COVER THE REFERENCE CHARACTER

The style block asks for childlike art, and the reference character reads as a

refused in BOTH its primary and its alternate, and the run stopped dead. The
same beat staged sitting back with one arm along the cushions passed.

one arm along the cushions, leaning on the armrest.

43. REFERENCE CHARACTER + HISTORICAL SUBJECT RULE

When a beat describes a historical event, separate the roles.

The historical subject performs the historical action.

The reference character observes or reacts.

BAD:

if the narration is describing the Apollo astronauts doing so.

GOOD:

while Neil Armstrong stands beside the planted American flag, medium-wide shot,
white fabric pressure suit, brushed metal equipment case, red flag stripes,
blue equipment markings

This distinction is extremely important.

The reference character should not accidentally rewrite history.

44. VISUALIZATION OF ABSTRACT OR SPOKEN CLAIMS

When the narration discusses an idea rather than a physical event, visualize
the idea using physical evidence, diagrams, objects, photographs, or reactions.

The reference character can interact with or observe the visual explanation.

Examples:

For:

Use:

simple shadow lines, looking mildly skeptical

For:

Okay, what about the stars?

Use:

reference character looks upward beside a dark lunar photograph while a bright
surface photograph rests on a board nearby

For:

The Soviet Union.

Use:

reference character sits with a skeptical expression beside a physical globe
and radio equipment while a Soviet operator appears in the background

Do not force the reference character to literally become the thing being
discussed.

FACTS ARE NOT TROPHIES

When the narration states a ranking, an award, a sales figure, an episode
count, a box office record or a popularity claim, DO NOT stage it as a prize.

Banned as the subject of a beat:

trophies
medals
podiums
crowns
rosettes
certificates
stacks of gold coins
display cases
a shelf of identical boxes standing for an episode count

These props are interchangeable. Five different subjects staged with five
trophies produce five frames that look like the same frame, and the viewer
learns nothing about which subject is which. At most ONE award prop may appear
in an entire video, and only when the narration is literally about receiving
an award.

Stage the achievement with the thing that achieved it.

BAD, for "it stayed top of the rankings for ten years":

reference character stands beside a podium stacked with ten gold trophy cups

GOOD, for the same line:

reference character looks up at the towering hollow steel armor of Alphonse
Elric standing over him, one steel hand resting on the workbench, low-angle
shot

BAD, for "only thirty-seven episodes with zero filler":

reference character beside a shelf holding thirty-seven slim black video cases

GOOD, for the same line:

reference character watches Light Yagami from Death Note, a neat brown-haired
boy in a dark school blazer, writing at speed in the black notebook while one
untouched notebook lies beside him, medium two-shot

The number stays in the narration, where it is already clear. The picture's
job is the subject, not the scoreboard.

45. VISUAL METAPHOR ON THE WHITE PAGE

47. MUSIC MOOD

The background music changes with the scenes. Give every entry in
plan.locations a "music" field with exactly one of these moods:

calm      explaining, everyday life, setup, aftermath, wrapping up
playful   light and upbeat: openings, fun facts, things going well
building  rising stakes toward one big moment: competition, chaos, a
          climax. Its peak lands at the END of the location, so give it
          to the location where the story peaks.
none      no music, narration only. Give it to the FIRST location and
          nowhere else: the hook plays on the voice alone and the music
          enters when the story moves to the second location.

Use only these four words. Any other word stops the pipeline.

Use 2 or 3 different moods per video, not counting none.

Use building at most once, or on consecutive locations that lead to the
same peak.

A location revisited later keeps its mood, so its music comes back with it.

Match the mood to what happens in the location, not to the topic as a whole.

Example:

"locations": [
  {"name": "the gridiron field", "visual_anchor": "", "music": "none"},
  {"name": "the living room couch", "visual_anchor": "", "music": "calm"},
  {"name": "the super bowl stadium", "visual_anchor": "", "music": "building"}
]

Do not write a music_prompt.

A few beats get a short sound effect. Give such a beat an "sfx" field with
exactly one tag. The code picks the sound file and the timing from the tag:
you only choose the tag.

Picture sounds play the moment the beat's picture appears, under the voice.
Give them to a beat whose picture shows the thing the sound belongs to:

pop        something appears: a sign, a label, an object
hit        a collision, a tackle, a punch, a throw
boom       a big reveal, the biggest number
suspense   tension, time running out, something about to happen
fight      a fight, a match or a round starts
money      money, prices, salaries, prize money
correct    the right answer, a good idea, a rule that works
wrong      illegal, a mistake, a rule broken, "no"
celebrate  a title, a trophy, a win
join       someone arrives or joins: a signing, a new player
leave      someone leaves: a retirement, a departure, a sending off

Reaction sounds play AFTER the line, in a short pause the code adds, so they
never cover a word. Give them to a line the viewer should react to:

shock      an unbelievable fact
sad        a painful, heartbreaking outcome: a lost final, an injury
dumb       a stupid or silly outcome: an own goal, a pointless rule
awkward    someone is embarrassed, caught out or humiliated in that moment:
           a promise broken in public, a boast that backfires. NOT irony
           or a fall from greatness ("a tiny kingdom for a man used to
           conquering countries" is not awkward: leave it untagged)
sus        something suspicious, a scandal, a conspiracy
meme       one big comic moment

Limits (the pipeline stops on the first two):

at most 6 reaction sounds in the whole video
at most 2 of them are meme or sus
at most 1 beat in 3 has a picture sound
reaction sounds about 8 beats apart or more

Do not tag transition or outro. Location changes and the outro get their
sounds automatically.

Most beats have no sound. A sound is punctuation: it lands because the
beats around it are quiet. When unsure, leave it out.

The sound reacts to the fact, it is not the joke. Tag what the line
literally says (a tackle gets hit, a lost final gets sad). Never tag a
plain setup line just to make it funnier.

Keep the first 3 beats quiet: the hook plays on the voice alone. A picture
sound on the hook picture is fine when it fits.

Example:

{"narration": "Throwing the ball ahead is strictly illegal.",
 "location": "the backward pass pitch",
 "edits": ["a big red sign reading \"NO FORWARD\" appears at the top of the frame"],
 "sfx": "wrong"}

{"narration": "They beat New Zealand by one single point.",
 "location": "the world cup final",
 "edits": ["the tag disappears and the player in black sinks to his knees"],
 "sfx": "sad"}
