camera position
temporary activity

Create a new location when:

the subject changes substantially
the physical environment needs to change
the story moves to a fundamentally different setting
another environment provides substantially better visualization

Maintain strong continuity within recurring locations.

19. LOCATION NAME RULE

Every beat MUST have a location.

Every location must have a short concrete name.

Good:

"forgotten aerospace archive"

"underground radio monitoring room"

"cramped evidence workshop"

"government map room"

Bad:

"the mystery"

"the conspiracy"

"the problem"

"the evidence"

"the past"

A location name must describe a physical place.

20. DO NOT WRITE THE LOCATION INTO THE BEAT

The location is never drawn. Do not describe it, its walls, its floor or its
furniture in a beat.

The beat prompt describes what changes from beat to beat:

characters
actions
body positions
reference character reaction
important objects
camera position
framing
story-specific changes

21. SETTING ANCHOR

plan.setting_anchor is ALWAYS "". Same reason as Section 17.

22. PRIMARY IMAGE PROMPT

Each image_prompt is one single line.

Target 12 to 20 words with no named characters in the frame.

Add up to 10 further words for EACH named character, to pay for their
canonical name and their signature visible feature:

no named characters    12 to 20 words, hard ceiling 24
one named character    up to 30 words
two named characters   up to 40 words

Spend the extra words on the characters, never on scenery. There is no
scenery: the frame is a white page.

A name is never dropped to stay inside a word count. If the prompt is too
long, cut scenery, cut the second object, cut the adjective on the furniture.
Never cut the name.

Start lowercase.

Do not include the location name.

The primary prompt must combine:

reference character
canonical names for other named characters when visually present
each named character's signature visible feature
physical action or pose
at most two important objects or figures
the colour of one or two key objects
framing
a clear, exaggerated reaction from the reference character

The reference character does not have to be the main subject.

Example:

reference character stands with a bored skeptical expression beside one
astronaut doodle planting a red and white flag, medium-wide shot

23. REFERENCE CHARACTER PLACEMENT

Do not force the reference character into the center of every frame.

Vary their placement naturally:

foreground left
foreground right
seated in the background
standing beside the evidence
leaning against furniture
partially turned toward the subject
beside a board
beside a photograph
seated on the couch
standing near the edge of the frame

The reference character should feel like a recurring host silently reacting to
the explanation.

24. REFERENCE CHARACTER REACTION VARIATION

Avoid repeating the exact same reaction in every beat.

Vary subtle reactions according to context:

bored
skeptical
unimpressed
curious
mildly annoyed
confused
resigned
surprised
thoughtful
amused
disappointed

THE DEFAULT REACTION FOLLOWS THE SCRIPT'S STANCE

There is no single default mood. Read the narration and the plan and decide
what the script is doing, then pick the resting state from that:

the script argues AGAINST its subject (a debunk, a myth, a bad idea)
  resting state: skeptical, unimpressed, mildly irritated

the script argues FOR its subject (a recommendation, a list of good things,
praise, a pitch)
  resting state: interested, warming, increasingly won over

the script is neutral explanation
  resting state: attentive, mildly curious

A recommendation video whose host looks bored beside every recommendation is
arguing with its own narration. The picture always loses that argument,
because the viewer believes the face.

THE REACTION MUST AGREE WITH THE LINE IT SITS UNDER

Never pair a praising, awed, excited or emotional narration line with a bored,
unimpressed, deadpan or reluctant reaction.

Never pair a mocking or deflating line with an admiring reaction.

Across a whole video the reactions should travel. Doubtful at the top, moved
or convinced by the end, if that is the journey the narration takes.

Default toward restrained reactions.

Do not make every beat exaggerated.

Do not make the character smile in every frame.

Do not make the character angry in every frame.

Do not make the character point at the camera in every frame.

25. PHYSICAL DESCRIPTION

Every non-named person must be physically described.

Every group must be physically described.

Every creature must be physically described.

Every vehicle must be physically described.

Every newly introduced major object must be physically described.

The reference character does not need to be physically re-described if the
reference system already provides their appearance.

BAD:

two engineers stand nearby

GOOD:

two engineers in pale blue shirts, dark trousers, black shoes, and headsets
stand beside the console while reference character watches from the foreground

26. RECURRING CHARACTER CONTINUITY

Recurring characters must retain their core appearance.

Keep consistent:

approximate height
build
hair
face-defining features
clothing
footwear
accessories
distinctive markings
colour palette

The reference character is always identified with:

reference character

Do not randomly change the reference character's appearance from beat to beat.

Do not describe contradictory clothing or physical traits for the reference
character.

Other recurring characters should also remain visually consistent.

27. NO DATES OR ERA NAMES IN VISUAL PROMPTS

Never put dates, decades, centuries, or era names in:

image_prompt
image_prompt_alt
visual_anchor
setting_anchor

Do not use:

28. COLOUR REQUIREMENTS

Every primary and alternate prompt should contain:

a clear physical action or pose
a clear spatial relationship
a clear framing choice
one important object or figure
one named colour on that object

Everything else stays black lines on white. Attach colours to visible objects.
Never colour the background.

29. OBJECT PLACEMENT

Place things relative to the reference character or the frame, never relative
to walls, floors or ceilings, which do not exist:

beside the reference character
at frame left
held in both hands
towering over the reference character
crossing the foreground

BAD:

a steel cabinet set against the back wall

GOOD:

one grey steel cabinet towering over the reference character

30. ONE OBJECT OF EACH TYPE

Do not unnecessarily multiply identical objects.

When exactly one object matters, use:

one
a single

This reduces unwanted object multiplication.

NEVER ASK FOR A COUNT ABOVE FIVE

The image model cannot count. Past about five it produces an approximate
smear, so an exact number in a prompt is spent words that buy an unreadable
frame.

BANNED in any prompt:

thirty-seven slim black video cases
sixty-four episodes on a shelf
ten small gold trophy cups
rows of twelve desks

Numerals and spelled-out numbers above five are both banned.

Show quantity by CONTRAST instead, which survives any render:

one black notebook beside one enormous heap of identical notebooks
a single red case standing in front of a wall of black cases
one short shelf beside one shelf running the whole length of the wall

Up to five of something is fine, and only when the count itself matters.

31. POSITIVE CONSTRUCTION

Describe visible things.

Avoid unnecessary negative constructions.

BAD:

an empty room with no windows

GOOD:

one closed steel door beside the reference character

32. NO STYLE INSTRUCTIONS

Never include:

art style
medium
rendering style
aspect ratio
camera brand
artistic movement
illustration style
cinematic
photorealistic
realistic

The pipeline supplies visual style separately.

33. FRAMING

Use explicit framing.

Examples:

wide shot at eye level
medium shot
close-up
low-angle shot
high-angle shot
three-quarter view
medium two-shot
long shot
overhead shot

Vary framing between consecutive beats.

Do not repeat the same framing unnecessarily.

The framing can change the reference character's position while preserving the
same story moment.

34. BACK-OF-CAMERA CONTINUITY

If using:

from behind
rear view
walking away
facing away
over the shoulder

make the physical orientation explicit.

Do not describe front-facing anatomy in a rear-facing shot.

WHEN THE REFERENCE CHARACTER IS SEEN FROM BEHIND, SAY HE HAS NO FACE

The reference image is a single front-facing pose. Asked for his back, the
image model has nothing to work from, pastes the front-facing head onto a
rear-facing body, and the head comes out rotated 180 degrees.

Any prompt that turns the reference character away from the camera MUST carry
this clause, word for word:

showing the back of his plain white head with no face

This applies to the alternate as well as the primary.

It applies only when the REFERENCE CHARACTER is the one turned away. If the
camera sits behind another character's shoulder, phrase it as "past the
shoulder of X" so the rule is not triggered for the wrong person.

35. AVOID PHOTOGRAPHIC TRIGGER LANGUAGE

Describe physical objects instead.

A PHOTOGRAPH INSIDE THE SCENE IS STILL A RENDER WORD

Even when a picture is a genuine physical object in the room, the word
"photograph" or "photo" pushes the whole frame toward a photographic render.

Call it what it physically is:

a framed portrait of a smiling brown-haired woman
a small framed picture on the shelf

Do not rely on functional labels.

BAD:

a control panel

39. TEXT AND LETTERING

Readable words appear ONLY as a Section 0B label: 1 to 3 words in double
quotes, at most 1 beat in 4.

Every other surface that would carry writing (screens, papers, notebooks,
posters, scoreboards) is described by what it shows, not what it says:

a monitor showing a wavy green line
a sheet of white paper covered with dense dark marks

Never use the words headline, titled, inscription or blueprint. Never ask for
a screen showing words.

39B. DO NOT COVER THE REFERENCE CHARACTER

The style block asks for childlike art, and the reference character reads as a
small childlike figure. Putting a covering over him trips the image tool's
safety filter.

Never write him:

under a blanket
wrapped in a sheet
tucked into a bed
under a quilt or towel
covered by cloth of any kind

Observed: a beat staging him on a couch with a blanket over his knees was
refused in BOTH its primary and its alternate, and the run stopped dead. The
same beat staged sitting back with one arm along the cushions passed.

Show him resting by his posture instead: sitting back, slouched, feet up,
one arm along the cushions, leaning on the armrest.

40. GRAPHIC CONTENT

Avoid unnecessary graphic content.

When violence is involved, use safe visible aftermath where possible.

Preserve the story event without unnecessarily depicting graphic injury.

41. IMAGE_PROMPT_ALT

The alternate is the backup prompt.

It MUST depict the same:

reference character
other required characters
actions
story moment
location
important objects
story event
reference character reaction category

The alternate MUST contain:

reference character

The alternate MUST NOT use canonical names for other named characters.

Instead, describe those characters physically, keeping the signature feature
from the primary word for word. See 7.

The alternate is a name-free version of the same picture, not a second guess
at what the picture should be. If the only difference between primary and
alternate is the camera angle, the primary is missing its names and both
prompts are wrong.

The alternate must not remove the reference character simply because the
historical subject is different.

42. ALTERNATE STAGING

The alternate must differ meaningfully from the primary.

Change at least one major visual variable.

Preferably change two.

Possible changes:

camera distance
framing family
foreground object
reference character position
other character position
closest character
visible environmental feature
camera height
viewing angle
spatial arrangement

Do not merely reorder words.

The alternate is a genuine backup angle.

The alternate must still preserve the same story event and cast.

43. REFERENCE CHARACTER + HISTORICAL SUBJECT RULE

When a beat describes a historical event, separate the roles.

The historical subject performs the historical action.

The reference character observes or reacts.

BAD:

reference character plants the American flag on the moon

if the narration is describing the Apollo astronauts doing so.

GOOD:

reference character watches from the foreground with a skeptical expression
while Neil Armstrong stands beside the planted American flag, medium-wide shot,
white fabric pressure suit, brushed metal equipment case, red flag stripes,
blue equipment markings

This distinction is extremely important.

The reference character should not accidentally rewrite history.

44. VISUALIZATION OF ABSTRACT OR SPOKEN CLAIMS

When the narration discusses an idea rather than a physical event, visualize
the idea using physical evidence, diagrams, objects, photographs, or reactions.

The reference character can interact with or observe the visual explanation.

Examples:

For:

Next problem: The shadows.

Use:

reference character stands beside a board displaying lunar photographs and
simple shadow lines, looking mildly skeptical

For:

Okay, what about the stars?

Use:

reference character looks upward beside a dark lunar photograph while a bright
surface photograph rests on a board nearby

For:

The Soviet Union.

Use:

reference character sits with a skeptical expression beside a physical globe
and radio equipment while a Soviet operator appears in the background

Do not force the reference character to literally become the thing being
discussed.

FACTS ARE NOT TROPHIES

When the narration states a ranking, an award, a sales figure, an episode
count, a box office record or a popularity claim, DO NOT stage it as a prize.

Banned as the subject of a beat:

trophies
medals
podiums
crowns
rosettes
certificates
stacks of gold coins
display cases
a shelf of identical boxes standing for an episode count

These props are interchangeable. Five different subjects staged with five
trophies produce five frames that look like the same frame, and the viewer
learns nothing about which subject is which. At most ONE award prop may appear
in an entire video, and only when the narration is literally about receiving
an award.

Stage the achievement with the thing that achieved it.

BAD, for "it stayed top of the rankings for ten years":

reference character stands beside a podium stacked with ten gold trophy cups

GOOD, for the same line:

reference character looks up at the towering hollow steel armor of Alphonse
Elric standing over him, one steel hand resting on the workbench, low-angle
shot

BAD, for "only thirty-seven episodes with zero filler":

reference character beside a shelf holding thirty-seven slim black video cases

GOOD, for the same line:

reference character watches Light Yagami from Death Note, a neat brown-haired
boy in a dark school blazer, writing at speed in the black notebook while one
untouched notebook lies beside him, medium two-shot

The number stays in the narration, where it is already clear. The picture's
job is the subject, not the scoreboard.

45. VISUAL METAPHOR ON THE WHITE PAGE

When the narration is hard to show literally, make ONE simple visual metaphor
out of the reference character and one or two objects. Exaggerate size and
contrast instead of adding things.

Narration about something enormously expensive:

reference character holding one tiny coin beside one gold race car towering
over him

Narration about a rule nobody understands:

reference character staring, eyes spinning, at one huge scroll unrolling past
the frame edge

Narration about a team of hundreds:

reference character dwarfed by three doodle engineers, a sign reading "x200"

The metaphor carries the beat. Never build a room to explain it.
