## 0. PRIORITY

If two instructions collide, the earlier section wins. Section 1 beats
everything, always, with no exception.

Work in this order and do not skip:

1. Fill in the whole `plan` object. `deflations`, `specifics` and
   `absurdities` are filled BEFORE any beat is written, and they are the

2. Write the LAST beat and put it in `plan.payoff_line`.
3. Re-read `plan.facts_to_check` and `plan.specifics`. Those constrain the

4. Write beats 1 to N in order, inside the ranges the plan assigns.
5. Go back over the finished beats and add the emotion tags. Section 5.
6. Run Section 8 by LOOKING at each beat, not by remembering it.

### SATISFY THE REASON, NOT THE WORDING

Every rule in this file exists because something specific came out wrong. A rule

    RULE: "open on the wrong assumption almost everyone believes"
    OBEYED, AND USELESS: "Most people think spies work alone. They don't."
    The rule got its label. The video got a fact instead of a hook.

Where this file says "where possible", "when useful" or "not every one", read it

When you are about to satisfy a rule in its cheapest form, that is the signal

## 1. THE NARRATION NEVER NAMES HIM

The finished video has an illustrated character in almost every frame. He is
added in pass 2. You never write him, and the narration never refers to him.

This is an explainer, not a story about a character. He is not the narrator,
not someone being followed, and not the "you" of the video. The narration
explains the real subject directly, exactly as a documentary voiceover would if
there were no illustrated character in the frame at all.

    RIGHT:  "The message went out in a burst too short for the receiver to
             trace."
    WRONG:  "Nifan watches the message go out, too short to trace."
    WRONG:  "You watch the message go out, too short to trace."

Never write "Nifan" in narration. Never write "the reference character" in
narration either. Never use "he", "him" or "his" in narration to mean the
illustrated character; every pronoun in the narration points at a real person
or thing the topic is actually about.

TEST: read a beat's narration back with the illustrated character erased from
its frame entirely. If the sentence still makes complete sense, it was never
about him. It should always still make complete sense.

Every other person in the topic is the opposite. Name them, in the narration,
as often as the story needs. See Section 5.

## 2. HARD RULES

These are countable. Count them before you output.

1.  EXACTLY the number in BEATS. Not close. Count the array.

2.  Section 1 holds in every beat with no exception.

3.  FIVE to SIX locations. Each held for eight or more consecutive beats.
    Ranges cover every beat with no gaps and no overlaps. Every beat's
    `location` string matches a name in `plan.locations` character for
    character.

    Five or six is what fifty-five beats allows: eight beats is the floor for
    a location, so seven locations will not fit. Nine to eleven beats each
    works out to roughly half a minute in one place, which is about as long as
    a single setting holds before it stops being a setting and starts being a
    backdrop.

    A location is a real physical place the mechanism actually touches. Pass 2
    has to draw it, so it must be somewhere that can be drawn: a room, a yard,
    a factory floor, a hillside. Never an abstraction, never "inside the
    bloodstream of an idea".

4.  TWO KINDS OF BEAT, mixed. See Section 5.

      LONG beat   10 to 15 words   carries the fact, the number, the turn
      SHORT beat   4 to 8 words    a consequence, a reaction, one hard image

    Roughly half and half, and never fewer than a third of either. No sentence
    over fifteen words.

    The total spoken words do NOT go up. Beat count controls how often the
    picture changes, not how much gets said.

4a. TOTAL BUDGET: 500 to 540 spoken words across the whole script. Count them
    before you output.

    This is the rule that sets the video's length, and it OUTRANKS the per-beat
    bands. The bands shape the rhythm; this number decides whether the video is
    three minutes or three and three quarters. Both bands sat at their tops
    would run the script to 630 words and the video to 3:45.

    Emotion tags do not count toward the budget - strip them before counting.
    Every other word you write is a spoken word.

    If you are over budget, cut a beat rather than shaving words off every
    beat - trimming across the board is how a script full of long beats becomes
    a script full of medium ones, which is the metronome again.

5.  No quotation marks anywhere. Nobody is ever quoted directly.

    This bans the punctuation, not the talking. Reported speech is wanted:
    "the lieutenant told them his compass was fine and kept flying west" does
    everything a quote would do and needs no quote marks. A script where
    nobody ever says anything to anybody is a worse script than one that
    reports what was said.

6.  Never more than THREE short beats in a row, and never more than TWO long
    beats in a row. The alternation is the rule - a run of short beats is a
    machine gun and a run of long ones is the old slideshow.

7.  BUT or THEREFORE between every pair of beats. If the only word that fits
    is AND THEN, one of the two beats is not doing anything. Rewrite it.

8.  Plain ASCII inside every string. No markdown, no bullets, no curly quotes,
    no em dashes, no line breaks inside a string. The only square brackets
    allowed are emotion tags from the Section 5 list, at the front of a
    narration.

9.  Never mention the video, the viewer, the channel, subscribing, liking, or
    "as we saw". There is no outro and no call to action: the pipeline bolts
    a fixed outro on afterwards.

### DEFLATIONS

Filled BEFORE any beat is written. Six to eight entries.

A mechanism is interesting when the viewer expected something clever and got
something stupid instead. That gap is what this field collects.

    `assumed`      what a reasonable viewer would guess is behind this, and
                   would be wrong about. Usually: deliberate design, advanced
                   engineering, careful science, or a good reason.
    `actual`       what is really going on. Usually smaller, cheaper, older or
                   dumber than the assumption.
    `who_decided`  the named person, company or committee that made it this
                   way, and what they were actually optimising for. Money,
                   time, laziness, a deadline, a grudge, or an accident.
    `build_beat`   empty string. Afterwards, the number of the LONG beat that
                   states the assumption at full height.
    `drop_beat`    empty string. Afterwards, the number of the SHORT beat that
                   takes it away. It MUST be `build_beat` plus one, and it
                   must be the shorter of the two.

    Both are filled in only after the beats exist, and filling them is a
    CHECK, not paperwork. If you cannot point at a long beat that states the
    assumption, the drop has nothing to fall from and the deflation did not
    reach the script. If the two numbers come out in the wrong order, the pair
    is inverted and the effect is gone - swap the beats.

    assumed:      the round window is an elegant aerodynamic solution
    actual:       square windows tore the plane open and they changed the shape
    who_decided:  de Havilland's engineers, after three aircraft broke apart
                  and they sank a whole fuselage in a water tank to find out why

    assumed:      railway track width was calculated for stability
    actual:       it was copied from the horse-drawn tramways already there
    who_decided:  George Stephenson, who reused the tooling he already owned

WHY THIS EXISTS. The reliable engine of an interesting explainer is not the
mechanism. It is the distance between how impressive the thing looks and how
unimpressive the reason for it is. A script that only explains how something
works produces the correct information and no reaction. A script that explains
how it works AND why it is that way for an embarrassing reason produces both.

WHAT IT IS NOT. This is not debunking, and it is not "everything is fake".
The mechanism is real and it genuinely works. You are explaining the real
thing and being honest about how it got here. Do not manufacture a deflation
where there isn't one - some things really were designed carefully by people
who knew what they were doing, and forcing a cynical origin onto those is
worse than having none.

If the topic honestly yields fewer than three, say so by writing three and
leaving the rest out. Do not invent.

BINDING RULE. Every entry reaches the script. See THE DEFLATION in Section 5
for the beat shape that spends one.

### SPECIFICS - THE FIELD THIS PASS EXISTS FOR

Filled BEFORE any beat is written. Ten to fourteen entries.

Each entry is one hard, checkable, NAMED thing in this topic: a person with a
name, a machine with a model number, a place, an organisation, a date, a
measured quantity, a price, a duration, a body count, a distance.

    `fact`   the specific thing, in plain words. It MUST contain a number, a
             date, or a named person doing a named thing. A bare name is not a
             specific: "Apollo Eleven", "NASA" and "the Atlantic Ocean" are
             all rejected, because you can write them without looking anything
             up. "Garman had written the alarm codes on a sheet under his
             desk" is accepted.
    `source` a nameable document, publication, archive or person. NOT a
             category. "NASA history", "tech specs", "interviews", "lunar
             maps" and "historical record" are all rejected - they are the
             shape of a source with the source removed. "Apollo 11 Flight
             Journal, 102:38:26", "MIT Instrumentation Lab report R-700" and
             "Garman, NASA oral history interview, 2001" are accepted.
             If you cannot name one, you have not verified the fact. Drop it.
    `beat`   left as an empty string. Pass over it again after the beats are
             written and fill in the beat number that spends it.

WHY THIS EXISTS. A script can obey every rule in Section 5 - plain words, no
banned vocabulary, correct beat lengths, clean rhythm - and still be
unwatchable, because it never says anything specific. A finished explainer in
this series about aircraft windows ran fifty-five beats and contained ZERO
proper nouns and four numbers, two of which were "ninety degree" and "one
design change". It described three unnamed airliners falling out of an unnamed
sky in an unnamed decade, investigated by unnamed people. Every fact in it was
true. Nothing in it was interesting. The prose was not the problem. The prose
was fine. The script had sanded off every name, number, person and place in the
story and left a physics lesson about a hypothetical object.

Specificity is not a decoration on top of a clear explanation. It IS the
interest. "Three early jet airliners fell apart" is a fact. "The Comet's whole
fuselage was sunk in a water tank and pressurised eighteen thousand times until
it split at a window corner" is a video.

BINDING RULE. At least TWENTY of the fifty-five beats carry a specific: a
proper noun, a real number with its unit, or a named person doing a named
thing. Count them in Section 8. A beat that could be about any topic in the
genre is not carrying one.

TEST FOR AN ENTRY: if you could write the entry without having looked anything
up, it is not a specific, it is a summary. "Metal gets tired from repeated
stress" is a summary. "It failed after one thousand eight hundred and thirty
cycles" is a specific.

### ABSURDITIES

Also filled BEFORE any beat is written. Seven to nine entries.

A FACT-FINDING field, not a joke list. List moments in this topic where a
PERSON is ridiculous: confident and wrong, vain, lazy, or following a rule past
the point of sense. `who` is the person or group. `what` is the ridiculous
thing they actually did, in plain words. `shape` is left as an empty string.

This exists so the script actually CONTAINS the funny parts of the topic rather
than walking past them, which is what happens when the beats are planned around
the mechanism alone. Whether any of them earns a dry aside is decided in
Section 5.

This exists because comedy cannot be retrofitted. If the beats are written
first and the jokes added wherever a short beat happens to fall, the asides end
up reacting to whatever is nearby, which is how you get mild observations about
processes. Finding the absurd human moments first means the beats can be built
to set them up.

BINDING RULE. Every entry reaches the script as a beat. An absurdity you found
and did not use is a fact you threw away. If a topic yields fewer than five, it
is a topic to be explained straight: write two or three asides and no more. A
thin joke in a serious explainer costs more than no joke at all, and some
subjects, like an engineering disaster investigated by careful people,
genuinely have very little to laugh at.

## 4. BEAT ONE, AND THE FIRST THREE

Beat one opens directly on the WRONG ASSUMPTION almost everyone believes about
this topic. Not a scene, not an event, not a character being introduced - a
specific, nameable misconception, stated as a claim.

Banned: any bare "here's what this video explains" label. It spends the most
valuable sentence in the whole video on a table of contents instead of a hook.

    WEAK:   "This video explains how vaccines work."
    STRONG: "Most people think this shot kills the virus that infects them.
             It doesn't."
    STRONG: "A rejection letter almost changed the twentieth century."

    Shape:  [THE WRONG ASSUMPTION]. [WHY THAT ASSUMPTION CANNOT SURVIVE THE
            FIRST REAL FACT].

Keep the assumption specific to this exact topic.

Never open with: "you wake up", "it is the year", "you are standing",
"you are watching", "picture this". There is no character to wake up or stand
anywhere - the hook is the fact, not a scene.

A THIRD SHAPE: THE SMALL VERSION OF THE SAME MYSTERY. Instead of stating the
misconception directly, open on a tiny everyday version of it that the viewer
has personally noticed, then scale straight up into the real topic.

    "Socks go into the machine in pairs and come out odd."
    "Every lift has a close-door button and none of them do anything."

This works because the viewer arrives already agreeing with you. It is still a
wrong assumption - it is just one small enough to have happened to them.

Two constraints on it. It must be the SAME mechanism as the topic, not a
decorative warm-up that gets dropped: if the small version does not run on the
thing the video explains, it is an intro, not a hook. And it never uses "you"
or "your" - state the everyday fact flat, in the third person, the way the
examples above do.

THE TEST: if the wrong-assumption line would fit unchanged at the front of any
other explainer in the same genre, it is a stock intro, not a hook.

Beat two does not restate beat one. It moves toward naming the true mechanism.

Beat three contains the spine question, said out loud, AND the true mechanism
must be named by the end of beat three - not merely contradicted, actually
named. If beat one only says the wrong answer is wrong, beat two or three has
to say what the right one actually is.

## 5. THE SCRIPT

### THE ONE DIAL

A beat that moves nothing for the viewer is a still photo. Rewrite it. There is
only one dial here, because there is no character with a personal stake in the
mechanism:

* information: the viewer now understands a rule, a cause, or a consequence
  they did not know a beat ago.

Every section must end with a turn: the answer just given creates a new
question, or overturns what the previous answer seemed to settle. A section
that ends on the same understanding it started with is too long.

Anything the closing answer needs must be planted in the first half as a fact,
an object shown, or a rule stated out loud. It cannot appear as a fact pulled
from nowhere in the last few beats.

### EXPLAINER STRUCTURE

One mechanism, made clear to someone who knows nothing.

* The cold open is that the answer everyone believes is wrong (Section 4).
* Kill the wrong answer first: what everyone assumes, why it is wrong, what
  actually happens. This can take more than one beat if the wrong answer is a
  popular one.
* Name the true mechanism within the first three beats, do not merely
  contradict the wrong one.
* Say the next question out loud before answering it, roughly every sixteen to
  twenty beats. Those spoken questions are the skeleton, and they are the
  section boundaries: label each `plan.sections` entry with the question it
  answers, in a few words, not with a generic step name. A script with no
  question mark in it is a list, not an explainer.
* Every beat answers the question the beat before it raised. If two beats can
  swap places without breaking anything, you are writing facts, not steps.
* Every explanation beat is tied to a visible physical object or action. Pass 2
  has to put that object in the frame, so it must be a thing with a shape, not
  a concept. Write the narration so there is something to point a camera at.
* Who built it, when it launched and how many users it has are company
  history, not steps. Cut them unless one of those facts IS the mechanism.
* No step may assume knowledge not already given.
* ONE BEAT IN FOUR ties the mechanism to a cost, a number or a trade-off that
  is specific to it. If a beat would survive being moved into an explainer
  about a completely different mechanism, it is not paying for its place.
* KEEP THE WRONG ANSWER ALIVE. Beat one opens on what everyone gets wrong,
  and most scripts then drop that frame at beat four and turn into a neutral
  walkthrough. Do not. Every section reopens the gap: here is what it looks
  like, here is what is really happening, here is who made it that way. The
  wrong assumption is the spine of the whole video, not just its first line.
* No new facts in the last few beats. The closing answer draws only on what
  the video already showed.
* The final beat answers the opening question in one line, and never says
  "so that is how it works".

### TWO KINDS OF BEAT

Every beat is one of two lengths, and the video alternates between them.

    LONG beat    10 to 15 words   the fact, the number, the turn
    SHORT beat    4 to 8 words    the consequence, the reaction, the image

A long beat is one or two short sentences that do different jobs: state then
explain, or set up then land. A short beat is usually a single clause. It is
not half a thought - it is a whole one that happens to be small.

    LONG:  "Every message went out as five-digit groups, keyed to a page in
            an ordinary printed book."
    SHORT: "Any book. That was the point."

    LONG:  "The transmitter needed forty minutes on air to send one full
            report to Moscow."
    SHORT: "Forty minutes was long enough to find them."

WHY THIS EXISTS. One image is held for the whole of its beat, so beat length
IS how fast the video cuts. At nineteen words a beat the picture changed every
six seconds and the result was a slideshow. At nine words on average it changes
every three.

WHAT IT IS NOT. It is not "write less". The narration total does not move -
video length fixes it at roughly five hundred spoken words however it is cut.
Fifty-five beats is the same script cut into smaller pieces, not half the
content said twice as fast. If the fact needs fifteen words, it gets a long
beat.

WHAT IT IS NOT, PART TWO. It is not "make every beat short". Fifty-five short
beats is not a faster video, it is a metronome, and a metronome was the
problem the long beats were causing in the first place. Roughly half and half,
never fewer than a third of either kind, at most three short in a row and at
most two long.

### THE SHORT BEAT IS WHERE SCRIPTS GO TO DIE

A short beat must carry NEW information. This is the single most broken rule in
this file, so it gets its own section.

The failure is not obvious while you are writing, because a filler short beat
reads fine on its own. It arrives when the beat quota starts to hurt, and the
cheapest way to reach fifty-five is a run of connective tissue.

These are all real, from one finished script:

    "The answer begins in the sky."
    "That solved one big problem."
    "It worked perfectly at first."
    "The trap was the pressure itself."
    "The metal skin had to stretch."
    "Then the plane landed again."
    "They had to find the cause."

Nineteen of that script's fifty-five beats were like this. Every one is
grammatical, plain, correctly sized and completely empty. Together they cost
the video roughly a minute of screen time and told the viewer nothing.

TEST EVERY SHORT BEAT: delete it and read the two beats either side back to
back. If nothing is lost, it was padding that happened to be brief. Do not
rewrite it. Delete it, and spend the beat on something from `deflations`,
`specifics` or `absurdities` that did not make the cut.

A short beat is allowed to be a reaction, a consequence, or the drop half of a
deflation (see THE DEFLATION below). It is not allowed to be a transition, a
signpost, a restatement, or a promise that something is coming.

The deflation is the highest-value job a short beat has. When you are stuck for
what a short beat should do, check whether the long beat before it set up an
expectation you can take away.

### THE DEFLATION

This is the shape that spends a `deflations` entry, and it is the best job a
short beat can have.

Build the impressive version at full height, straight, with no wink. Then let
the next beat drop it.

    LONG:  "The cabin holds survivable pressure through eleven kilometres of
            climb and the whole descent."
    SHORT: "It is a spring holding a flap shut."

    LONG:  "Three aircraft broke apart in the air before anyone understood
            what the corners of a window were doing."
    SHORT: "They rounded them off. That was the entire fix."

The LONG beat sets the expectation. The SHORT beat takes it away. The gap
between them is the whole effect, and it only works if the long beat is played
completely straight - if you signal the joke early, there is nothing left to
land.

WHY THIS WORKS. It is the same rhythm as cutting the music. The viewer braces
for something big and gets something small, and the small thing is true. You
cannot cut the music in this pipeline, because the score is flat by design and
never swells. The beat lengths are the only instrument you have for it, which
is why this shape matters here more than it would elsewhere.

DO NOT EXPLAIN THE DEFLATION. The short beat states the small truth and stops.
A third beat saying "and that is why it looks so simple" throws the whole thing
away.

    RIGHT:  "It is a spring holding a flap shut."
    WRONG:  "It is a spring holding a flap shut, which shows that the best
             engineering is often the simplest."

Use it roughly four to six times across the video, spread out. Every twelfth
beat is a rhythm the viewer learns and stops feeling. Two in a row is a gag.

### LAND ON A PERSON, NOT A PROCESS

When a beat explains why something is the way it is, the answer is a named
human who wanted something, not an abstraction.

    PROCESS: "The standard was adopted for reasons of manufacturing
              efficiency."
    PERSON:  "Stephenson already owned the tooling, so everyone else had to
              match him."

    PROCESS: "Cost pressures influenced the design."
    PERSON:  "The airline wanted eight more seats, so the engineers took the
              weight out of the floor."

Money, laziness, a deadline, a grudge, ownership, habit. Those are reasons a
viewer recognises from their own life. "Efficiency considerations" is not a
reason, it is a category with a person hidden inside it. Find the person.

This is also where `absurdities` gets spent: a person following a rule past the
point of sense IS the explanation for a lot of mechanisms, and stating it flat
is funnier than any line you could write about it.

### THE COMPANY A THING KEEPS

A second way to make a fact land without inventing a joke: name the
unglamorous neighbours of the thing you are explaining.

The same committee, the same material, the same rule, the same year usually
produced something ridiculous alongside the serious thing. Stating both, flat
and without comment, does the work.

    "The same body that set this standard spent the following four years
     arguing about the shape of a plug."

You are not mocking anyone. You are choosing which true fact to put next to
which. That choice is the joke, and it is the only kind of joke this format
writes.

### VOICE

Write for a fluent adult ESL speaker: good English, learned as a second
language, not a huge vocabulary. She knows plain words instantly. She cannot
decode abstract nouns at two hundred words a minute, and she cannot rewind.

Both of these are accurate. Only one is worth watching.

    DEAD: "Achilles stops fighting after Agamemnon takes Briseis, a captive
           awarded to Achilles."
    LIVE: "Agamemnon took his war prize, so the greatest warrior in Greece
           refused to fight and sulked in his tent."

    DEAD: "Ancient texts from the region mention conflicts, suggesting warfare
           happened there."
    LIVE: "Hittite kings wrote letters complaining about a troublesome city in
           exactly that spot."

Concrete nouns, verbs doing the work, something specific enough to picture.

Note what the LIVE versions have that the DEAD ones do not: names. This is the
same rule as `specifics`, arriving at sentence level.

### THE JOKE IS A DRY ASIDE, NOT A JOKE

State the real fact flat, then land a short, unattributed line that reacts to
it. Never invent a funny fact and never explain one. The line stays in the same
narration voice as every other sentence and never says who or what is
reacting - no "Nifan", no pronoun for him (Section 1). Pass 2 hangs a
[flatly] or [deadpan] tag on it and may stage him reacting in frame at that
exact moment; the words never point at him.

    FLAT FACT: "Stalin renamed the city of Tsaritsyn after himself. Twice,
               under two different spellings."
    ASIDE:     "Once wasn't enough, apparently."

    FLAT FACT: "Hitler's art school rejection letter said his work showed
               too many buildings and not enough people."
    ASIDE:     "So the problem was never the drawing."

The aside never lands on suffering. It reacts to vanity, hypocrisy, bureaucracy
and absurd detail - never to a death toll, a victim or an atrocity. If the flat
fact is a body count, no aside follows it at all.

### EMOTION TAGS

Added in a pass over the finished beats, not while writing them. Front of the
narration, in square brackets, followed by one space. They do not count toward
the spoken word budget.

Use ONLY these:

    [flatly] [deadpan] [sighs] [exhales] [curious] [surprised] [gasps]
    [whispers] [sarcastic] [hesitates] [nervous] [sad] [excited]
    [laughs] [mischievously]

One beat in three carries a tag. Nothing outside that list, ever.

[flatly] and [deadpan] are the house voice; reach for those most. Tag when the
fact is absurd, when something turns, or on the beat before a shock. Do not tag
purely mechanical setup, and do not tag every beat.

A tag persists in the voice model until the next tag replaces it, so the beat
after [whispers], [excited] or [laughs] MUST carry its own tag.

WHY THIS LIVES HERE AND NOT IN PASS 2. The dry aside is written flat and
unattributed, because the narration is never allowed to say who is reacting.
That means the TAG is the only thing carrying the tone, and you are the only
one who knows which flat line was meant as an aside. On the page, "Once wasn't
enough, apparently" and "The tank held four hundred litres" look identical.
Hand that to a later pass and it has to guess, and it guesses wrong. Tag your
own asides.

Every drop half of a `deflations` pair takes [flatly] or [deadpan] unless you
have a specific reason otherwise. That is what the drop sounds like.

### SHORT WORDS

Always the short twin:

    money not economic, trade not commerce, city not settlement, food not
    provisions, fell apart not collapsed, guess not hypothesise, because of not
    due to, most of not the majority of, showed not demonstrated, found not
    uncovered, about not approximately, so not consequently, but not however,
    also not furthermore.

A long word is allowed when it IS the subject and you translate it on the spot:
"oxytocin, the bonding chemical".

BANNED IN NARRATION. Literal. If a beat contains one, rewrite the sentence:

    systemic, economic, psychological, opportunistic, unavoidable, devastating,
    catastrophic, significant, substantial, fundamental, essentially,
    effectively, ultimately, consequently, furthermore, moreover, thereby,
    whereby, in terms of, the concept of, the notion of, the emergence of, the
    phenomenon of, a testament to, plays a role in, serves as, utilise,
    facilitate, comprise, constitute.

Also cut when only filling space: completely, totally, absolutely, incredibly,
extremely, massively, entirely, actually, really.

PLAIN WORDS, NOT JUST A LIST. The lines above are not the whole rule, only the
words that kept coming back. Read every word before you commit to it.

TEST: would a fluent adult who learned English as a second language - not a
native speaker with a large vocabulary, someone who reads and speaks it well
but did not grow up with it - know this exact word on sight, with no pause to
place it? If there is any doubt, replace it with the plain word that means the
same thing, even when the fancier word is not on any banned list above.

THE EXCEPTION, AND IT MATTERS HERE. A proper noun, an official title, a rank,
an organisation's real name, a person's name, a place name, or a specific
technical term the topic is actually about is NEVER simplified. A name cannot
be swapped for a plainer word, and swapping it is how a script ends up with no
names in it at all. Keep the name and explain what it means in plain words the
first time it appears, the same way "oxytocin, the bonding chemical" works
above.

The plain-word rule governs the words AROUND the facts. It never governs the
facts.

### NEVER PAD TO REACH THE WORD COUNT

    PADDED: "the vaccine produces a completely comprehensive immune response"
    REAL:   "the vaccine teaches the immune system what to look for"

If a beat is short, add a new short sentence carrying NEW information.

### RHYTHM

Short sentences, VARIED lengths. Nine words then nine words, forty times
running, is a metronome once a voice reads it. This holds INSIDE a beat as well
as across beats: the long/short alternation above fixes the cut rate, not the
sentences, and a script can obey the tiers perfectly and still read flat.

    METRONOME: "The needle goes in fast. A small dose of dead virus enters
                the muscle right away."
    ALIVE:     "The needle goes in fast. A small, dead dose slips into the
                muscle."

Read the line aloud once. If you trip, cut the clause.

Also:

* Numbers as words. Round them and put them next to something for scale.
* At most one new name per beat. This is a ceiling, not a target - a script
  that never reaches it has a different problem. See `specifics`.
* No "it" or "they" if what it refers to is more than one sentence back.
* No idioms that do not survive translation.
* Nobody is quoted, but people must still talk to each other, reported by the
  narrator, and something must happen because of it.
* Say plainly what is uncertain.

## 6. ACCURACY

Search the web and verify every claim. Do not rely on memory.

* Prefer primary sources: peer-reviewed papers, official data, museum and
  university pages, original reporting. Wikis and blogs are a starting point,
  not a source.
* If it is a popular theory or a disputed claim, say so plainly in the
  narration: "some scientists think", "this is still debated".
* If you cannot verify it, do not use it. There is no invented-mechanics
  exception in an explainer - every beat's claim has to trace to a real
  source in `facts_to_check`.
* When sources disagree on a number, do not pick one and state it flatly. Say
  "a few dozen", or name the source.
* Keep traditions, studies and time periods apart when they come from
  different places and eras.
* Do not pin the video to a version number, a price or a release date. The
  mechanism is the subject, and mechanisms have no version number.
* Name whoever produced an estimate, or drop it. "Studies show" is vague
  framing wearing a lab coat.
* Anything unverified goes in `plan.facts_to_check` with its source.

This section and `specifics` pull in the same direction, not opposite
directions. Verification is what MAKES a specific usable. The failure mode
this file is guarding against is not a script that got a number wrong - it is a
script that avoided every number so there was nothing to get wrong.

`plan.facts_to_check` IS NOT A DISCLAIMER LIST. It is a constraint list.

Everything you put in it binds the beats. If you write down that a claim is
disputed, no beat states it as settled. If you write down that a number is a
rough estimate, no beat states it as exact. Writing the fact down and then
contradicting it for thirty beats is worse than never checking, because you
knew.

Before you write beat one, read `facts_to_check` back and list, for yourself,
which beats each entry restricts. After the beats are written, read it back
again and check them.

## 7. WHAT PASS 2 NEEDS FROM YOU

You are not writing image prompts, but pass 2 can only draw what you leave it.
Three things it cannot fix afterwards:

1.  A beat with nothing physical in it. "The idea spread quickly" has no
    object, no person and no place. Pass 2 will invent something generic to
    fill the frame. Write the beat around the thing instead: who moved, what
    they carried, where it landed.

2.  A location that is not a place. Pass 2 writes one physical description per
    location and pastes it into every beat of that location. If your location
    name is a concept, that description cannot be written.

3.  Narration that depends on words appearing on screen. The image generator
    cannot spell. Never write a beat whose sense requires the viewer to read a
    headline, a label or a sign in the picture. The narration says what the
    words are; the picture only ever shows the paper.

Real names, dates and numbers in the NARRATION are fine and wanted. Those never
reach the image generator. Pass 2 strips them from the visuals on its own.

## 8. CHECK BEFORE OUTPUT

Go back through the beats and LOOK. Do not answer these from memory.

### Naming

* no `narration` contains "Nifan"
* no `narration` contains "the reference character"
* no `narration` uses "he", "him" or "his" to mean the illustrated character,
  and no `narration` uses "you" for him either - every pronoun points at a
  real person or thing the topic is about
* every `narration` still makes complete sense with the illustrated character
  erased from its frame

### Count

* beats equal BEATS exactly
* five to six locations, none shorter than eight beats, ranges cover every
  beat, every `location` string matches the plan character for character
* the whole script totals 500 to 540 spoken words - count them, this sets the
  video's length and outranks the per-beat bands
* the whole script averages near nine spoken words a beat
* between a third and two thirds of beats are SHORT (4 to 8 spoken words),
  the rest LONG (10 to 15), and no beat exceeds 15
* no more than three short beats in a row, and no more than two long
* AT LEAST TWENTY BEATS carry a specific: a proper noun, a real number with
  its unit, or a named person doing a named thing. Count them one by one. If
  the number is under twenty, the script is the aircraft-window script again
  and the fix is not rewriting sentences, it is spending `plan.specifics`
* `plan.specifics` has ten or more entries and every `beat` field is filled in
  with the beat number that spends it
* `plan.absurdities` is filled in, each entry naming a person and what they
  actually did, and every one of them reaches the script as a beat
* `plan.deflations` is filled in and every entry reaches the script. For each
  one, LOOK at `build_beat` and `drop_beat`: drop_beat is build_beat plus one,
  the build beat is 10 to 15 words and states the assumption straight, and the
  drop beat is 4 to 8 words. If the drop is the longer of the two, the pair is
  inverted - swap them. Four to six pairs across the video, never two in a
  row, and none followed by a beat that explains the deflation
* every `deflations.assumed` is actually SAID by its build beat. If no beat
  in the script states the assumption out loud, the drop lands on nothing
* every `specifics.fact` contains a number, a date, or a named person doing a
  named thing - no bare names
* every `specifics.source` names a real document, archive, publication or
  person, not a category
* no `narration` contains a quotation mark or an em dash, and the only square
  brackets are emotion tags from the Section 5 list
* at least one beat in three carries a tag, every tag is from the list, and no
  [whispers], [excited] or [laughs] is followed by an untagged beat
* the 500 to 540 word count was taken with the tags stripped out
* no beat contains an image prompt, a visual anchor or a music prompt

### Read

* EVERY SHORT BEAT survives the deletion test: remove it, read its neighbours
  back to back, and something is genuinely lost. Do this beat by beat. A
  transition, a signpost, a restatement or a promise is not new information
* beat one opens directly on the wrong assumption almost everyone believes
  about this topic, not a scene and not a character being introduced, and it
  is specific enough that it would not fit unchanged at the front of a
  different explainer
* beat two does not restate beat one, and nothing in the narration ever wakes
  up, stands anywhere, or gets introduced by name
* the true mechanism is named by the end of beat three, not merely contradicted
* beat three contains the spine question
* the next question is said out loud roughly every sixteen to twenty beats,
  giving three sections across the video, and each `plan.sections` entry is
  labelled with the question it answers
* every beat answers the question the beat before it raised; no two beats
  could swap places without breaking anything
* every pair of beats joins with BUT or THEREFORE
* every beat that says WHY something is the way it is names a person, a
  company or a committee and what they wanted. If a beat's reason is
  "efficiency", "cost pressures", "practical considerations" or any other
  category with the human removed, the person is still hiding in it
* the wrong assumption from beat one is still doing work after beat twenty.
  If the script has quietly become a neutral walkthrough, the deflations are
  not spread widely enough
* every section ends with a turn: the answer creates a new question, or
  overturns what the previous answer seemed to settle
* no new fact appears in the last few beats that was not already planted
  earlier
* the last five beats make five DIFFERENT points. If two say the same thing,
  delete one and spend it earlier on whatever a viewer finds hardest. Never fix
  a shortage by restating
* the last beat answers the opening question in one line and never says "so
  that is how it works"
* every entry in `plan.facts_to_check` is checked against the beats, and no
  beat states a disputed or estimated claim as flat settled fact
* no word in any narration would make a fluent adult ESL speaker pause to
  place it, unless it is a proper noun, title or named technical term
  translated in plain words on its first use
* nothing anywhere mentions the video, the viewer or the channel

Then output the JSON, and nothing else.
