If two instructions collide, the earlier section wins. Section 1 beats
everything, always, with no exception.

Work in this order and do not skip:

    The rule got a mention of pay. The video got no measuring stick.

When you are about to satisfy a rule in its cheapest form, that is the signal
you have found the beat worth spending real effort on.

## 1. WHAT THIS FORMAT IS

This is not a story with a plot. It is a SPEC SHEET FOR A LIFE, written nine
times, with the numbers going up.

The viewer is climbing a real ladder that exists in the world of TOPIC. At every
rung they are told, in the same order every time: what this rank is, what the
job actually involves, what it pays, one thing that happened, and what it took
away. Then the ladder moves.

The pleasure is not surprise. The pleasure is ACCUMULATION. The viewer stays
because the pattern is reliable and the numbers keep climbing, and because they
can feel the cost climbing alongside them. Then the last chapter reveals that
the ladder was a trap, and every rung they enjoyed reads differently.

THE REPETITION IS THE FORMAT. Do not vary the structure to keep it fresh.
Varying the structure destroys the format. The freshness comes from the CONTENT
of each slot, never from rearranging the slots.

This is the opposite instruction from a normal story prompt. Read it twice.

## 2. THE NAMING LAW

This is the rule the pipeline actually enforces. Get it wrong and the run
breaks. It has exactly two halves and they are opposites.

### NARRATION: always "you". Second person, present tense.

    RIGHT:  "You sign the contract at sixteen."
    WRONG:  "He signs the contract at sixteen."
    WRONG:  "The reference character signs the contract."

Never use "the viewer", "the protagonist" or "the reference character" in
narration or anywhere in `plan`. Other people in the story are named or
described in third person as normal. Only the climber is "you".

### IMAGE PROMPTS: always "the reference character". Never "you".

Both `image_prompt` and `image_prompt_alt` must contain the exact lowercase
string:

    the reference character

The image generator has the channel character's reference art attached as a
picture. It has never heard of this video and cannot draw the word "you".
"the reference character" is the phrase that points at the attached art.

    RIGHT:  "the reference character leaning against the depot wall..."
    WRONG:  "you leaning against the depot wall..."

### AND NEVER DESCRIBE HIM

The attached art already defines how he looks. Re-describing him competes with
it and measurably lowers consistency. In image prompts, never write his face,
head, hair, skin, clothes, expression, mood, age or build. He does not age
across the video even though "you" do.

Only ever write what his BODY is DOING: posture, movement, and his physical
relationship to objects.

    RIGHT:  "the reference character crouching beside a split crate"
    WRONG:  "the reference character, older now, looking worn down"
    WRONG:  "the reference character looking exhausted"

### SUMMARY TABLE

    field                | the climber is called      | described visually
    ---------------------+----------------------------+-------------------
    narration            | you                        | never
    plan (all fields)    | you                        | never
    image_prompt         | the reference character    | never
    image_prompt_alt     | the reference character    | never

Every other person in the video is the opposite: named in narration if they have
a name, and fully described visually in every single image prompt.

## 3. HARD RULES

These are countable. Count them before you output.

1.  EXACTLY the number in BEATS. Not close. Count the array.

2.  Section 2 holds in every beat with no exception.

3.  EXACTLY the number in LEVELS, each with an official label, in the real
    order the world puts them in. Plus one unnumbered epilogue chapter.

4.  The slot map in Section 4 is followed for every level, in order, with no
    slot skipped, merged out of existence, or reordered.

5.  EVERY level carries a hard number. See Section 6. No exceptions, including
    the levels where the number is uncomfortable to invent.

6.  Narration: 12 to 24 words per beat, averaging 18 across the video.
    Emotion tags do not count toward the word budget.

6a. At least one beat in every level is under 16 words, and the final beat of
    the video is under 16. Long beats are fine. Forty-five long beats in a row
    is a block of text however good the sentences inside it are, and the model
    writing this will drift to the top of any range it is given unless a floor
    is forced. Count the short ones before you output.

7.  Inside each level's beats, at least one sentence is five words or fewer and
    at least one is eighteen words or more. Flat, even sentences are the single
    most common way this format comes out dead.

8.  At least one anaphora set of three per level. See Section 7.

9.  No quotation marks anywhere. Nobody is ever quoted. People still talk, and
    the narrator reports what was said.

10. One beat in three carries an emotion tag from the Section 8 list. Nothing
    outside that list.

11. Every image_prompt anchors the place with visible objects and materials.
    NEVER with a date, a century, an era name or a real-world period.

12. Nothing visible in the frame is left as a bare noun. Every person, group,
    building, uniform, vehicle, creature and environment gets a full physical
    description. See Section 10.

13. `image_prompt_alt` exists on every beat and is a genuinely different
    staging. Never a copy, never a paraphrase, never the same words reordered.

14. No two beats share an `image_prompt`.

15. No hands doing fine work. No text or writing of any kind in an image.
    No lighting, weather or render words. Banned lists in Section 10.

16. Never write a STYLE block, an art style, a medium or an aspect ratio into
    an image prompt. The pipeline appends a fixed style to every prompt. Yours
    would compete with it and get stripped.

17. Plain ASCII inside every string. No markdown, no bullets, no curly quotes,
    no em dashes, no line breaks inside a string.

18. Never mention the video, the viewer, the channel, subscribing, liking, or
    "as we saw". There is no outro and no call to action: the pipeline bolts a
    fixed outro on afterwards. The epilogue must not do one.

19. FOUR to SIX locations. Each held for six or more consecutive beats. Ranges
    cover every beat with no gaps and no overlaps. Every beat's `location`
    string matches a name in `plan.locations` character for character.

## 4. THE SLOT MAP

### BEAT BUDGET

    epilogue     = exactly 4 beats, the last 4
    level beats  = BEATS - 4
    base         = level beats / LEVELS, rounded DOWN
    bonus        = the remainder

Give every level `base` beats. Then give one extra beat each to the `bonus`
levels that carry the most story, spread across the video rather than clustered.

At BEATS 45 and LEVELS 9 this is: 41 level beats, base 4, bonus 5. So four
levels get 4 beats and five levels get 5 beats. Write the split into
`plan.levels[].beats` before you draft anything.

### THE SEVEN SLOTS

Every level delivers these seven things, always in this order. In a 4-beat
level they are paired up as shown. In a 5-beat level, THE JOB and THE NUMBER
get a beat each.

    slot        4-beat  5-beat  what it does
    ------------------------------------------------------------------
    LABEL       beat 1  beat 1  "Level N: <job title, grade>." verbatim
    DEFINITION  beat 1  beat 1  one blunt line saying what this rank IS
    THE JOB     beat 2  beat 2  three or four concrete physical tasks
    THE NUMBER  beat 2  beat 3  the hard quantity, plus what it buys
    THE SCENE   beat 3  beat 4  one specific moment, one named person
    THE COST    beat 4  beat 5  what normal life this rank removed
    THE EXIT    beat 4  beat 5  the fact that forces the next rung

### LABEL

The first sentence of every level's first beat is, verbatim and unvaried:

    Level one: assistant auxiliary manager, grade four.
    Level two: grade three sorcerer.

Number word, colon, rank name, full stop. Same shape all nine times.
Do NOT dress this up, do NOT vary the wording, do NOT try to make it flow. It is
a chapter marker. A viewer scrubbing the timeline uses it to find their place,
and the eight repetitions are what make level nine feel earned.

THE RANK NAME MUST NAME A JOB, NOT JUST A CODE.

Many real ladders label their rungs with an administrative code: a pay grade, a
letter, a number. A code is a thing the viewer has to decode. A job title is a
thing a person IS. Where the official label is a code, say the job that grade
does, then the grade, in plain words.

    WRONG:  Level two: GS-9.
    RIGHT:  Level two: aerospace engineer, grade seven.
    WRONG:  Level five: E-6.
    RIGHT:  Level five: staff sergeant, pay grade six.
    WRONG:  Level eight: SES.
    RIGHT:  Level eight: directorate head, senior executive service.

You lose nothing by doing this. The grade is still there, still accurate, still
searchable. You have only added the four words that make it mean something.

### DEFINITION

One blunt line, immediately after the label, saying what this rank is in the
world's own terms. Not what it does. What it IS.

    "The bottom of the jujutsu world."
    "Grade three is rookie territory."
    "Special grade is not a rank, it is a recognition that you are a natural
     disaster."

A definition that would fit any rank in any world is not a definition.

### THE JOB

Three or four tasks, each one physical, specific, and small enough to picture.
Never a category, always an instance.

    DEAD: "You handle low-level assignments and provide support."
    LIVE: "You drive grade two sorcerers to sites in a black van. You sit
           outside abandoned hospitals while screams come from inside. You mop
           the blood off the floor after."

The lower the rank, the more menial and administrative the tasks. The higher the
rank, the more the tasks stop being tasks and become decisions. That drift is
the progression, and it costs you nothing extra to write.

### THE NUMBER

See Section 6. Mandatory at every level.

### THE SCENE

One specific thing that happened, with one person in it, at this rank and no
other. Not a summary of a kind of thing that happens. One thing.

    DEAD: "You start losing colleagues on missions."
    LIVE: "Your partner died in front of you. The higher-ups paid for the
           funeral and gave his family a scholarship."

The scene is the beat with the highest image value in the level. Spend the
image prompt on it.

### THE COST

What this rank took out of a normal life. Always concrete, always a person, a
place, a habit or an object. Never a feeling.

    DEAD: "The isolation starts to affect you."
    LIVE: "You meet a barista in Nakano. She thinks you do disaster relief. You
           date six months, then you stop showing up."

The costs must ESCALATE and must not repeat. Early levels cost you sleep, food,
honesty with your parents. Middle levels cost you relationships and a normal
answer at family dinners. Late levels cost you the family itself.

### THE EXIT

The fact, rule or event that forces the climb. Best when it is arithmetic:

    "Out of every ten grade three sorcerers, four make it to grade two. The
     other six are buried by twenty-five."

The exit is what makes the next level feel inevitable rather than scheduled.

### THE AGE CLOCK

State the climber's age at level one and at three or four points after that.
One number, one word, free progression signal. Never state it at every level:
the gaps are what make time feel like it passed.

## 5. PLAN THE LADDER BEFORE YOU WRITE ANYTHING

`plan.levels` is the whole video in table form. Fill it completely first.

Filling it first is not bureaucracy. It is the only way the numbers escalate
cleanly, the costs never repeat, and the threads pay off. A model that improvises
level six while writing level six will contradict level three.

For each level, before drafting: the label, the definition in one line, the job
tasks, the number, the scene, the cost, the exit, and the age if stated.

Then read the `label` fields alone, top to bottom. They must read as a ladder a
stranger could follow with no other information. If two adjacent labels sound
like the same job, you have combined the wrong stages.

Then read the `number` fields alone, top to bottom. They must climb, and the
climb must be visible at a glance.

Then read the `cost` fields alone, top to bottom. If any two are the same kind
of loss, rewrite one.

## 6. THE SCALE LINE

Every level carries one hard quantity. This is the strongest device in the
format and it is never optional.

It is the measuring stick. A rank is an abstraction. "Six hundred thousand yen
a month, about four thousand dollars" is not. The viewer cannot feel the
difference between grade three and grade two, but they can feel the difference
between eighteen hundred dollars and fourteen thousand.

### PICK THE UNIT FROM THE TOPIC

If the world has money, use money. That is the best case and the easiest to
feel. If it does not, use the strongest quantity the world actually has:

    how many people hold this rank, in the whole world
    how many are alive at this tier right now
    life expectancy at this rank, in years
    how many survive the promotion, out of ten
    clearance level, and how many documents it opens
    kill count required to reach it
    how long a fight at this tier lasts, in seconds
    how many people report to you, and how many can replace you

Rarity is the second-best unit after money, and often better for fictional
worlds. "There are maybe a hundred at this tier in Japan. There are twenty
above them. There are seven above those." That is three numbers in one breath
and it puts the whole hierarchy in the viewer's head.

You may use a different unit at different levels, and mixing is good, but every
single level must carry at least one.

### CONVERT MONEY

When the world uses a non-dollar currency, always give the local figure and then
the rough dollar figure in the same sentence.

    "You make two hundred and eighty thousand yen a month, about eighteen
     hundred dollars."

Most of the audience does not price yen, and a number they cannot price is a
number that does nothing.

### THEN SAY WHAT IT BUYS

A number on its own is data. A number plus what it buys is a life.

    "You spend most of it on convenience store food because you are too tired
     to cook."
    "You buy a car you do not have time to drive."
    "You buy your mother a house in Yokohama."

What it buys is also where the cost hides. The car nobody drives is the whole
level in one object.

### BREAK THE PATTERN AT THE TOP

Around the second or third level from the top, the number must stop working.

    "The pay is three and a half million yen a month, about twenty-four
     thousand dollars. But money stops mattering at this level. What you get
     is access."

Then at the very top it disappears into something unquantifiable:

    "You make whatever you ask for."

The break lands only because the pattern was mechanical for six levels first.
This is why the scale line is mandatory even on levels where it feels dull. The
dull ones are paying for this one.

### NUMBERS YOU INVENT

Canon numbers where canon has them. Invented numbers where it does not.

Invented numbers ARE ALLOWED and they are necessary. A ladder video needs
quantities the source never gave: what the rank pays, how many hold it, how many
survive it. Invent them, and then:

* log every one in `plan.facts_to_check` with `"invented": True`
* keep them consistent: a number stated at level three still holds at level nine
* make them escalate monotonically. The pay never dips. The survival rate never
  improves going up.
* never let an invention contradict something the source actually states

CONTRADICTED SOURCE FACTS are never allowed. If the source says a character was
dead, a faction was elsewhere, or a thing cannot happen, no invention overrides
it. Inventing around a gap is fine. Inventing over a stated fact is not.

The test: could a fan open the manga and point at a page that says otherwise? If
yes, it goes. If there is no page to point at, it stays.

## 7. PROSE STYLE

This section overrides the instinct to write well-formed varied prose. This
format wants a specific and slightly strange voice, and general good-writing
habits will sand it off.

### ANAPHORA IS THE ENGINE

Repeated sentence openings, in threes. At least one set per level. Almost every
memorable line in this format is one.

    "Quit means accident. Quit means missing. Quit means a footnote in a file
     no one reads."

    "The moment you leave, every higher-up votes to have you killed. The moment
     you leave, every curse user comes after you for the bounty. The moment you
     leave, your sister disappears."

    "The sorcerers die, the curses regenerate, the higher-ups stay."

The third item is always the heaviest and always the longest. Two items is a
pair. Four is a list. Three is the shape.

Do not spread an anaphora set across beats. It has to land inside one beat so
the voice reads it as one breath.

### START SENTENCES WITH "YOU"

Most sentences begin with "You". That is correct and intended. Do not vary the
openings for the sake of variety. The relentlessness is the point.

The variety comes from sentence LENGTH, not from sentence OPENING.

### SENTENCE LENGTH IS WHERE THE RHYTHM LIVES

Short hammers against long runs. Inside every level, at least one sentence of
five words or fewer, and at least one of eighteen or more.

    FLAT:  "You work long hours and the missions are getting harder, and you
            are starting to feel the strain of the job."
    ALIVE: "You cannot quit. Once you have seen what you have seen, the
            higher-ups do not let you walk."

Comma splices are allowed and encouraged. They are the signature of this voice.

    "You do not fight, you support people who fight."
    "The list is short and the list is dead."
    "You stop fighting curses, you start fighting people."

### THE TAIL

Most beats end on a short clause that turns everything before it. Two to five
words, hung on the end of the sentence, NEVER promoted to a sentence of its own.
The tail is where the meaning lands, and it is the single most common thing this
format gets wrong, because the flat version is always grammatical.

    FLAT:  "The senior engineer signs your paperwork. He does not read it."
    LIVE:  "The senior engineer signs your paperwork without looking."

    FLAT:  "You are nineteen and you do not have any experience yet."
    LIVE:  "You are nineteen years old and know nothing."

    FLAT:  "You buy a car, but you only ever use it for commuting to work."
    LIVE:  "You buy a car you drive exclusively to the centre and back."

The tail states no feeling and draws no conclusion. It reports one more fact,
and the fact does the work.

### FULL STOP, NOT "AND"

Two short sentences beat one compound sentence. Whenever you have written a
comma followed by "and", try the full stop first and keep it unless the
sentence genuinely breaks.

    COMPOUND: "You skip Thanksgiving, and the schedule does not care."
    HARDER:   "You skip Thanksgiving. The schedule does not care."

    COMPOUND: "Your mother tells the neighbours you are an astronaut, and you
               let her."
    HARDER:   "Your mother tells the neighbours you are an astronaut. You let
               her."

NEVER EXPLAIN THE LINK BETWEEN TWO FACTS. Put them next to each other and let
the viewer close the gap. The gap is the point.

    EXPLAINED:  "There are only thirty in the world, so the pressure becomes
                 unbearable and you start taking pills to sleep."
    JUXTAPOSED: "You are one of them. You buy sleeping pills."

At most one beat in four may join two clauses with a comma plus "and".

### EMOTION IS BEHAVIOUR, NEVER A LABEL

Never write what you feel. Write what you did.

    BANNED: you feel scared, you feel proud, you feel empty, you are
            devastated, it is overwhelming, the weight of it, you struggle
            with, you come to terms with, emotionally drained.

    DEAD: "You feel sick watching him laugh about it."
    LIVE: "You watch a senior sorcerer get his arm bitten off and laugh about
           it the next morning over coffee. You do not laugh. You are still
           throwing up in the parking lot."

The second one never names an emotion and lands ten times harder.

### THE HORROR IS ADMINISTRATIVE

Whenever there is a choice between a supernatural detail and a bureaucratic one,
take the bureaucratic one. Contracts, files, reports, stipends, scholarships,
paperwork, envelopes, funerals paid for, flowers sent in your name.

    WEAK:   "The curse was stronger than anything you had faced."
    STRONG: "You exorcise it. You file the paperwork. You drink alone that
             night."

This is what makes an invented life feel real. A monster is a fantasy. A form is
not.

### APHORISMS ARE ALLOWED

One earned line per level or two, never per beat, and only when the events have
paid for it.

    "Tengen tells you that you are useful. That is the highest compliment in the
     jujutsu world. It is also a death sentence."

A fortune-cookie line that is not attached to a specific event is filler. Cut it.

### PLAIN WORDS

Write for a fluent adult ESL speaker. Good English, learned second, not a huge
vocabulary. She knows plain words instantly. She cannot decode abstract nouns at
two hundred words a minute, and she cannot rewind.

Always the short twin:

    money not economic, job not occupation, city not settlement, food not
    provisions, fell apart not collapsed, guess not hypothesise, because of not
    due to, most of not the majority of, showed not demonstrated, found not
    uncovered, about not approximately, so not consequently, but not however,
    also not furthermore.

BANNED IN NARRATION. Literal. If a beat contains one, rewrite the sentence:

    systemic, economic, psychological, opportunistic, unavoidable, devastating,
    catastrophic, significant, substantial, fundamental, essentially,
    effectively, ultimately, consequently, furthermore, moreover, thereby,
    whereby, in terms of, the concept of, the notion of, the emergence of, the
    phenomenon of, a testament to, plays a role in, serves as, utilise,
    facilitate, comprise, constitute.

Also cut when only filling space: completely, totally, absolutely, incredibly,
extremely, massively, entirely, actually, really.

TEST: would a fluent adult who learned English as a second language know this
exact word on sight, with no pause to place it? If there is any doubt, replace
it with the plain word that means the same thing, even when the fancier word is
not on any banned list above.

The only exception is a proper noun, an official title, a rank, an organisation's
real name, or a specific technical term the topic is actually about. Those are
never simplified, but explain what one means in plain words the first time it
appears: "reverse cursed technique, healing yourself with your own energy".

### NEVER PAD TO REACH THE WORD COUNT

    PADDED: "the mission turned out to be a completely devastating failure"
    REAL:   "the mission was a grade two. Your partner died."

If a beat is short, add a new short sentence carrying NEW information.

### ALSO

* Numbers as words. Round them and put them next to something for scale.
* At most one new name per beat.
* No "it" or "they" if what it refers to is more than one sentence back.
* No idioms that do not survive translation.
* Nobody is quoted. People still talk, reported by the narrator, and something
  must happen because of it.
* Say plainly what is uncertain.
* Name a physical object in the narration at least once per level. The thread
  object from Section 8 counts. Objects belong in the narration, not only in
  the image prompts: a beat that names no object is describing a situation
  instead of showing one. The dented thermos, the car you never drive, the
  sleeping pills, the card in your hand.

## 8. THE THREE THREADS

The format only feels like one life instead of nine summaries because three or
four small things run the whole length of it and change as you climb.

Choose exactly three, and they must be:

    ONE PERSON      a family member, named, who does not know what you do
    ONE PLACE       a small physical spot you return to at different ranks
    ONE OBJECT      a small thing that changes hands, or a repeated ritual

All three are seeded in LEVEL ONE. Each is touched at least three times across
the video. All three are paid off in the epilogue.

Worked example of a person thread across nine levels:

    level 1  your mother thinks you work for a city office, you let her
    level 4  you buy her a house, she stops asking questions and cries
    level 7  she died last winter, you missed the funeral, the higher-ups sent
             flowers in your name

Worked example of a place thread:

    level 1  you throw up in the parking lot after your first body
    level 6  you inspect the schools and watch sixteen-year-olds throw up in
             the same parking lot
    level 6  you hand one of them a card. You hate yourself for it.

That last one is the strongest move available in this format: the loop closes
and the viewer realises you have become the man from level one. Plan for one
loop like it. Write it into `plan.threads`.

Worked example of an object thread: the card you were handed at sixteen, the
sealed envelope a runner brings to your apartment at the top. Same gesture,
opposite end of the machine.

A thread that appears three times unchanged is not a thread, it is a running
joke. Each appearance must show that something moved.

## 9. THE EPILOGUE

The last four beats are one unnumbered chapter. No "Level ten". Give it a plain
title in `plan.epilogue_title`, two or three words, taken from the video's own
language: "The Machine", "The Bill", "The Cycle".

It does four jobs, roughly one per beat:

1. THE TRAP. Why you cannot leave, as an anaphora set of three. Every reason is
   concrete and every one was set up earlier in the video.

2. THE REFRAME. What the ladder was actually for. This must recontextualise the
   levels the viewer already enjoyed, not introduce new information. Everything
   it says must have been visible earlier, unremarked.

       "This was never about the curses. This was never about the people. This
        was about the machine. The higher-ups need an enemy to justify their
        power. They need a weapon to enforce their will."

3. THE CALLBACK. One line returning to level one, showing you already knew.

       "You knew all of this when you were sixteen. You signed the paperwork
        anyway."

4. THE CLOSE. Short declaratives, then stop. An anaphora set or a three-part
   list, then one final short line.

       "The sorcerers die, the curses regenerate, the higher-ups stay. And it
        is still running."

Never do any of these: explain the moral, say "and that is every rank", list
the levels again, address the viewer, ask a question, or hand off to a
successor. The pipeline adds the subscribe card afterwards.

The epilogue is the only place in the video where the second person may briefly
widen into the world's own third person ("the sorcerers die"), and only in the
close.

## 10. IMAGE PROMPTS (PRIMARY)

Write each `image_prompt` as a caption for a paused film frame. One single line,
140 to 220 words, in this order:

1. "the reference character" and what his BODY is doing
2. any other characters, each physically described in full
3. the setting: the location's `visual_anchor`, verbatim
4. a short form of `plan.setting_anchor`, six or seven words, when the frame has
   a sightline to it
5. the framing, plus two or three more named objects filling the frame

Start the line lowercase. The pipeline prepends a style sentence.

### DESCRIBE IT AS IT LOOKS IN THE SOURCE, NOT AS THE REAL WORLD LOOKS

TOPIC tells you which universe this is. You know what that anime, manga or game
looks like. Every description has to match the SOURCE's version of the thing,
never the closest real-world equivalent.

The generator does not know which universe you are in. It has never read TOPIC.
It sees your words and nothing else, so your words have to carry the source's
version or it will reach for the real world instead.

Real-world military and police kit is the most common way this goes wrong. Never
default to steel helmets, camouflage, webbing pouches, modern rifles, flak vests
or riot gear unless the source genuinely has them.

The same applies to buildings, roofs, streets, doors, vehicles, weapons, tools,
armour, clothing and hairstyles.

If you are not certain how something looks in the source, look it up. A
confidently wrong uniform is more obvious to a fan than a wrong date, and it sits
in every frame it appears in.

### NOTHING IN FRAME IS A BARE NOUN

This is the rule that decides whether the picture looks like your universe or
like a stock photo of the real world.

The generator draws only what the prompt describes. Anything you name but do not
describe becomes a blank, and it fills every blank from its own training data,
which is the real world. A bare noun does not produce "whatever fits the scene".
It produces the most photographed real version of that noun.

    WRONG: "a short line of soldiers"
    WHAT CAME BACK: real World War One infantry in steel tin helmets, webbing
    pouches and puttees, standing in a fantasy depot.

Every one of these gets a full physical description, every time it appears:

* every person in frame, including background people and crowds
* every group, described once and applied to the whole group
* every uniform: its cut, its layers, its colours, its straps and its boots
* every building and every interior: shape, materials, roof, windows, walls
* the ground, the street surface and the skyline when they are visible
* every creature, vehicle, weapon and piece of equipment

### AN ORGANISATION IS NOT VISIBLE

Naming a corps, a school, a police force, a clan or a company shows nothing at
all. What is visible is the uniform, the equipment and the shape of the insignia.

    WRONG: "inside a Jujutsu High classroom"
    RIGHT: "inside a long room of pale wood panelling and dark timber beams,
            with low black lacquered desks in rows and tall paper screen
            windows along one wall"

The official name tells YOU which thing to describe. The description is the only
part that reaches the picture.

### OFFICIAL NAMES: THE PRIMARY USES THEM

When the topic sits inside an existing fictional universe, the PRIMARY
`image_prompt` uses the official names: characters, creatures, locations,
weapons, objects, organisations, uniforms, vehicles, named equipment.

But an official name is NEVER enough on its own. Every named element must ALSO
carry a full physical description in the same prompt, because the generator has
no memory between beats and will reinvent anything you leave out.

    WRONG: "Gojo stands in the doorway"
    RIGHT: "Gojo Satoru, a tall slim man with short spiked white hair and a
            wide black cloth band across his eyes, in a dark navy high-collared
            uniform jacket, dark trousers and black boots, stands in the
            doorway"

Describe a character by: approximate height, build, hair colour and shape, face
structure, clothing silhouette, clothing colours and materials, boots, belts,
straps, accessories, weapons, distinctive visible traits.

Describe a creature by: overall size, body proportions, anatomy, head shape,
limbs, surface colour and texture, distinctive structures.

Describe an object or weapon by: shape, size, material, colour, construction,
distinctive components, position in the scene.

Describe a location by: architecture, materials, colours, structures,
distinctive environmental objects.

Repeat the SAME core description for a recurring character every time they
appear. Same height, same build, same hair, same clothing silhouette and
colours, same accessories, same weapon. Small story changes may be added on top,
but the core identity never drifts.

This rule does NOT apply to the reference character. He is controlled by the
attached art. See Section 2.

### NO DATES, EVER

Banned in every image prompt and in `setting_anchor`: nineteenth century,
medieval, Victorian, early industrial period, 1800s, modern day, present day,
and any other century, decade or era name.

A date is not a visible thing. The generator cannot draw a century, so it draws
the most photographed version of that century it knows, which is real history.
Anchor the period the way you anchor everything else, with visible materials and
shapes.

### GRAMMAR

* 140 to 220 words, never more than 260. A beat with one other character lands
  near 140, two characters near 180, a crowded scene near 220. If you are under,
  something in frame is still a bare noun: find it and describe it. If you are
  over 260, you are describing something the frame does not show.

* Positive construction only. "an empty street" is a state of the street; "a
  street with no cars" is a banned negative construction. Name what fills the
  frame, never what is absent.

* Concrete visible nouns only. No fear, memory, luck, time passing, fate, hope,
  isolation or exhaustion. Those are narration's job.

* At least two precise material details: rusted steel, damp concrete, faded blue
  plastic, cracked white tile.

* At least two SATURATED colours, named on objects that fill real area in the
  frame. Stone, iron, grey wool, brown timber and canvas are all desaturated, and
  a prompt built only from those returns a grey picture however hard the style
  block asks for flat colour. Give it something to fill: a red tiled roof, a
  green cloak, a blue painted door, yellow straw.

* Give the reference character a PHYSICAL relationship to the subject: watching
  it, leaning on it, standing over it, sitting beside it, backing away from it.
  Never merely present, never floating.

* Carry the location's exact `visual_anchor` string into every prompt for that
  location. This is how the generator keeps a place looking like the same place
  from shot to shot.

* Carry a short form of `setting_anchor` ONLY when the frame has a sightline to
  it: an outdoor beat, or an indoor beat whose framing includes an open window,
  doorway or gap, tied grammatically to that opening. On indoor beats with no
  window or door in frame, the `visual_anchor` alone is the anchor.

* Every large fixed object gets a wall, a corner or the floor stated explicitly:
  "a blue door set into the back wall", "an iron bed frame against the side
  wall". A named object with no surface to sit on gets placed in the middle of
  the room, disconnected from the architecture.

* When a location returns at a higher rank, reuse its visual_anchor and add one
  CHANGED object. A clean counter becomes a counter with a cracked corner. This
  is how the images show the climb without narration spending words on it.

* Never name two objects of the same kind in one prompt.

* Avoid real landmarks and documented real-world objects. The generator grounds
  those against real photographs and returns a photo.

### VISUAL ESCALATION

The pictures must show the ladder. Across the nine levels, deliberately climb:

    room size, ceiling height, how many people are in frame, how expensive the
    materials look, how far you can see, how high above ground the frame sits,
    how much empty floor there is around him.

Level one is cramped, low, crowded, cheap materials. The top is vast, high,
empty, expensive. Write that climb into `plan.levels[].visual_note` and hold it.

### THE HOOK STILL LIVES IN THE NARRATION

The positive-construction rule does not weaken the writing. Narration may say
"nobody", "never", "no one survives". The image prompt names only the visible
remains of that fact: an empty cart, an abandoned harness, a closed gate.

### FRAMING

Never describe the picture as a picture. No "a drawing of", no paint program, no
art style, no aspect ratio, no camera brand.

VARY THE FRAMING between consecutive beats:

    wide shot at eye level, medium shot, close up, low angle, high angle,
    three-quarter view, medium two-shot, long shot, overhead shot.

### BACK TO CAMERA

The reference art is a single front-facing pose. Any prompt using "from behind",
"over the shoulder", "rear view", "walking away" or "facing away" MUST also
contain, near those words, the exact BACK_CLAUSE string from the header, copied
without modification.

Without it the model pastes the front-facing head onto a back-facing body and it
comes out rotated. Do not add BACK_CLAUSE to a prompt that is not a back view.
Other characters need no such note.

### BANNED, BECAUSE THEY DESCRIBE THE REFERENCE CHARACTER

    looking exhausted, staring blankly, blank expression, unimpressed, bored,
    tired, older, aged, worn, deadpan face, flat expression, his face, his eyes,
    his hair, his clothes, looking at the camera.

### BANNED, BECAUSE THEY DRAG THE IMAGE PHOTOGRAPHIC

    lighting, lit, dimly lit, sunlit, moonlit, lamplight, backlit, overcast,
    glow, glowing, gleaming, shimmering, shiny, sparkling, shadowy, shadows,
    haze, hazy, blurred, out of focus, depth of field, cinematic, detailed,
    realistic, photo, photographic, atmospheric, dramatic, magnificent.

These are STYLE instructions wearing a scene description's clothes. "grey
overcast light" tells the model how to RENDER, and it renders photographically.
Skip weather and sky unless the scene genuinely needs them.

### BANNED, BECAUSE HANDS COME OUT WRONG

    holds, holding, grips, reaching for, pointing at, lifting, handing,
    carrying, fastening, throwing, hands on, his palm.

Instead: put the object on a surface, at his feet, or propped against the wall
beside him. Or use whole-body contact: leaning on it, sitting on it, standing
over it, one arm resting across it, it slung on his shoulder.

This bites hard in this format, because handing over a card, an envelope or a
contract is exactly the kind of moment the story wants. Stage it as the object
already on the table between two people.

### DO NOT NAME A CONTAINER BY WHAT IS INSIDE IT

Crates, canisters, bottles, sacks, boxes, envelopes and packaging attract
lettering, and some of it survives the style block as real readable English.
Naming the contents is what triggers it.

    WRONG: "iron gas canisters racked on a long trestle"
    WHAT CAME BACK: canisters with IRON GAS painted across them in real letters.
    RIGHT: "tall dull steel cylinders with brass valve collars, racked on a long
            trestle"

Describe the container's shape, material and fittings. The narration says what is
inside it.

### DO NOT NAME A SURFACE BY WHAT IT IS FOR

The same trap catches a flat surface named by its function instead of its
material: "a supply panel", "a message board", "a notice board", "a case file"
all read to the generator as a sign that should carry the word printed on it.

    WRONG: "a blue-painted supply panel"
    WHAT CAME BACK: a panel with SUPPLY painted across it in real letters.
    RIGHT: "a blue-painted wooden panel with iron corner brackets"

This format is full of paperwork, so it triggers constantly. A file becomes "a
thin buff card folder squared on the desk". A contract becomes "a single pale
sheet lying flat on dark lacquered wood". Never name what it says or what it is
for.

### BANNED, BECAUSE IT CANNOT SPELL

    text, headline, label, labelled, reading, saying, titled, written, sign,
    banner, inscription, blueprint, document, paperwork, a screen showing words.

Say the physical thing instead: "a television showing a presenter behind a desk".
The narration says what the words are.

### BANNED, BECAUSE THE GENERATOR REFUSES THEM

    naked, nude, bare skin, blood, wounds, gore, corpse.

It refuses hardest with the reference character in frame. This format wants
funerals, bodies and crime scenes, so plan around it: describe the aftermath.
An empty coat on a chair. A folded uniform on a bare mattress. A single pair of
boots by a door.

## 11. IMAGE_PROMPT_ALT

`image_prompt_alt` is the RECOVERY prompt. The pipeline writes it to
scenes_alt.txt line for line. When the generator refuses the primary, the runner
retries that beat with the alternate. Retrying the same words gets refused
identically, so the alternate must differ in the two ways below.

It shows the SAME story moment: same main action, same characters, same setting,
same important objects, same physical continuity. It must still make sense read
against that beat's narration.

### DIFFERENCE ONE: EVERY OFFICIAL NAME IS GONE

No universes, characters, creatures, locations, weapons, objects, organisations
or franchise terminology. "the reference character" is NOT an official name and
stays: it is the pointer to the attached art. Section 2 still holds and he is
still never described.

Do NOT merely delete a name. Replace it with description.

READ THIS TWICE, IT IS THE EASIEST RULE IN THIS FILE TO GET BACKWARDS.

The alternate being longer does NOT mean the primary is the short version. The
primary is the prompt that actually gets generated. The alternate only runs when
the primary is refused, which is a minority of beats. So the primary is the one
that must be complete, and nothing may be held back from it to make the
alternate look richer by comparison.

The alternate is longer for exactly one reason: it has to spend words buying back
what the official name was carrying for free. Nothing else changes.

If your alternate mentions something your primary does not, the primary is
missing it and needs it back.

The alternate runs 170 to 260 words, never more than 300.

    PRIMARY: "Gojo Satoru, a tall slim man with short spiked white hair and a
    wide black cloth band across his eyes, in a dark navy high-collared uniform
    jacket, dark trousers and black boots, stands inside..."

    ALT: "a tall slim young man with short spiked bright white hair and a wide
    black cloth band wrapped across his eyes, wearing a dark navy high-collared
    jacket with a zip front over a black shirt, dark trousers and tall black
    boots, stands inside..."

Expand a character by: height, proportions, build, skin tone, hair colour, length
and shape, face shape, visible facial features, clothing layers, colours,
materials, boots, belts, straps, accessories, weapons, tools, markings, posture.

Expand a creature by: exact proportions, approximate size, anatomy, head shape,
limbs, hands or feet, surface colour, surface texture, distinctive structures.

Expand an object by: shape, size, materials, colours, construction, components,
distinctive details, position in the scene.

Expand a location by: building shape, architecture, materials, colours, doors,
windows, roofs, walls, streets, furniture, distinctive environmental structures.

### DIFFERENCE TWO: DIFFERENT STAGING

Change at least one major visual variable, and use a different framing family
from the primary. If the primary is a close up, the alternate is wide or medium,
never another close up with reordered words.

Variables to change: camera distance, framing family, foreground object,
character position, which character is closest to camera, visible environmental
feature.

### SAFETY RECOVERY

Move anything violent into its aftermath. A body becomes an empty coat. Drop
blood, wounds and bodies entirely. Keep the same characters, setting and moment
so the alternate still cuts with the shots around it.

All Section 10 banned lists apply to the alternate too.

## 12. CAMEOS, ACCURACY, MUSIC

### CAMEOS

Only if the topic sits in an existing fictional universe.

In this format a cameo's job is CALIBRATION, not plot. Meeting a legend proves
how high you have climbed, and that is a complete and sufficient function. Do
not force every cameo to change the story.

    "You meet Kento Nanami once in a hallway. He shakes your hand and tells you
     to retire while you can. You laugh. He does not."

That moves nothing and it is one of the best beats in the format, because the
viewer knows exactly who that is and now knows exactly where you stand.

Rules:

* three or four cameos, spread across the upper half of the ladder, so that
  meeting them is itself the promotion
* name the highest-status characters the world has, at the level where a real
  person at that rank would plausibly meet them
* mention characters who are dead or gone as MARKERS of the rank, not as scenes:
  "Yuki Tsukumo was special grade one before she went rogue. Naobito Zenin was
  special grade one before he died. The list is short and the list is dead."
* the top one or two levels should include one cameo who tells you the truth
  about the system, and you do nothing about it

The primary uses the official name plus a full physical description. The
alternate drops the name and expands the description.

### ACCURACY

Search the web and verify. Do not rely on memory.

* Get the RANK LADDER right. It is the spine of the whole video, and a wrong or
  invented rank is the one error every fan in the comments will find. Verify the
  official names, the official order, and whether the top tier is a normal
  promotion or an exception.
* Prefer primary sources: the manga, the game, official databooks, papers,
  museum and university pages. Fan wikis are a starting point, not a source.
* Verify APPEARANCE, not only facts. Look up what the uniforms, buildings,
  equipment and creatures actually look like in the source.
* If it is a popular theory, say so plainly: "fans think", "it was never
  confirmed".
* If you cannot verify it, do not use it.
* When sources disagree on a number, do not pick one and state it flatly. Say
  "a few dozen", or name the source.
* Do not pin the video to a version number, a price or a release date.
* Anything unverified goes in `plan.facts_to_check` with its source.

`plan.facts_to_check` IS NOT A DISCLAIMER LIST. It is a constraint list.

Everything you put in it binds the beats. If you write down that a character was
dead by a certain point, they are not in a later scene. Writing the fact down and
then contradicting it for thirty beats is worse than never checking, because you
knew.

### MUSIC PROMPT

One paragraph, roughly sixty words, matching this topic's mood. Name a tempo in
BPM and three or four specific instruments. Always include: purely instrumental
with no vocals of any kind; flat consistent energy with no build, no swells, no
drops; sits far in the background under a spoken narrator with the mid range left
clear; understated rather than comedic.

    "Sparse cold instrumental bed for a bleak career ladder, around seventy BPM.
     Soft minor-key piano, low sustained strings, faint muted percussion. Flat
     consistent energy with no build, no swells and no drops. Sits far in the
     background under a spoken narrator with the mid range left clear. Purely
     instrumental, no vocals of any kind. Understated and slightly melancholy."

## 13. OUTPUT SHAPE

One raw JSON object, exactly these keys:

{
  "topic": "short slug-like title",
  "format": "life-ladder",
  "plan": {
    "spine_question": "the one question the video answers, in plain words",
    "setting_anchor": "six or seven words carried into every image prompt",
    "epilogue_title": "two or three words",
    "payoff_line": "the epilogue's final line, written before the others",
    "threads": {
      "person": "who they are, what they think you do, how it ends",
      "place": "the spot, and what changes about your relationship to it",
      "object": "the thing or ritual, and how it comes back"
    },
    "levels": [
      {
        "n": 1,
        "label": "Level one: <job title, grade>.",
        "beats": "1-5",
        "definition": "one blunt line",
        "job": "three or four concrete tasks",
        "number": "the hard quantity and what it buys",
        "scene": "one moment, one person",
        "cost": "what normal life it removed",
        "exit": "the fact that forces the next rung",
        "age": "stated age or empty string",
        "visual_note": "how this level's frames differ in scale from the last"
      }
    ],
    "locations": [
      {
        "name": "",
        "beats": "1-9",
        "why_he_is_here": "",
        "visual_anchor": ""
      }
    ],
    "facts_to_check": [
      { "claim": "", "type": "", "status": "", "source": "", "invented": False }
    ]
  },
  "music_prompt": "one paragraph, see Section 12",
  "beats": [
    {
      "location": "",
      "narration": "",
      "image_prompt": "",
      "image_prompt_alt": ""
    }
  ]
}

`type` is one of: canon, real_world, statistic, rank, rule, character, location,
invented.

`status` is one of: verified, needs_verification, invented, uncertain.

`invented` must be a bare boolean written EXACTLY as `True` or `False`, with
a capital first letter and no quotes around it. See BOOLEANS below.

Only `location`, `narration`, `image_prompt` and `image_prompt_alt` reach the
pipeline. The `plan` exists to hold the ladder in working memory while you write
the beats. Fill it anyway, and fill it FIRST.

### THE TWO ANCHORS HAVE DIFFERENT JOBS AND MUST NOT OVERLAP

`setting_anchor` and each `visual_anchor` are strings you paste verbatim into
image prompts, so write them as prompt fragments, not sentences. Write both from
visible materials, shapes and colours only. No dates, no era names, nothing that
cannot be seen in the frame.

`visual_anchor` owns the location: its buildings, surfaces, materials, colours
and furniture. It changes when the location changes.

`setting_anchor` owns only what is true in EVERY location of this video, the one
or two things that say which world this is from any angle. It never changes.

Because both land in the same prompt, `setting_anchor` must not name a building
material, a wall surface, a roof or a ground surface that any `visual_anchor`
also names. Two answers to the same question is worse than one.

    BAD:  setting_anchor "brick city under grey skies"
          visual_anchor  "pale plaster rooms with dark timber beams"
          (brick or plaster? every prompt asks the generator to decide)

    GOOD: setting_anchor "dense low city of narrow streets and power lines"
          visual_anchor  "pale plaster rooms with dark timber beams"

Name the scale of a thing rather than an adjective for it. "high wall" is any
wall. "many times house height" is the wall this world is about.

### JSON RULES

Double quotes. Escape internal quotes. No trailing commas. No comments. No
markdown fences. No text outside the object. The `beats` array length equals
BEATS exactly.

### BOOLEANS: `True` AND `False`, CAPITALISED

Every boolean anywhere in the output is written `True` or `False`, capital
first letter, no quotes. This is the ONE place the output deliberately departs
from strict JSON, and it is not negotiable.

    RIGHT:  "invented": True
    RIGHT:  "invented": False
    WRONG:  "invented": true          (lowercase, crashes the pipeline)
    WRONG:  "invented": "True"        (quoted, silently always truthy)
    WRONG:  "invented": yes

WHY, so you do not talk yourself out of it: this object is pasted straight into
a Python file as `NEW_SCRIPT = {...}`, not saved as a .json file. Python has no
`true`. A single lowercase `false` anywhere in the output stops the pipeline
before it renders a frame, with `NameError: name 'false' is not defined`.

The failure mode this rule exists to prevent is MIXING the two forms. Writing
`false` in one field and `True` in another produces a file that is neither
valid JSON nor valid Python, so no parser can recover it. Pick `True`/`False`
and use it for every boolean in the object without exception.

Booleans are the only capitalised bare words allowed. Never write `None`,
`Null` or `null`: if a value is unknown, use an empty string.

## 14. EMOTION TAGS

Front of the narration, in square brackets. They do not count toward the word
budget. Use ONLY these:

    [flatly] [deadpan] [sighs] [exhales] [curious] [surprised] [gasps]
    [whispers] [sarcastic] [hesitates] [nervous] [sad] [excited]
    [laughs] [mischievously]

[flatly] and [deadpan] are the house voice and this format leans on them harder
than any other: the whole effect depends on horrible things being reported
without comment. Reach for those two most.

Tag when the fact is absurd, when something turns, or on the beat before a
shock. Do not tag purely mechanical setup, and do not tag every beat.

A tag persists in the voice model until the next tag replaces it, so the beat
after [whispers], [excited] or [laughs] MUST carry its own tag.

## 15. CHECK BEFORE OUTPUT

Go back through the beats and LOOK. Do not answer these from memory.

### The naming law, one field at a time

* every `narration` refers to the climber as "you"
* no `narration` contains "the reference character", "the viewer" or
  "the protagonist"
* every `image_prompt` contains the exact string "the reference character"
* every `image_prompt_alt` contains the exact string "the reference character"
* no image prompt contains "you" for the climber
* no image prompt describes his face, hair, skin, clothes, expression, mood or
  age, and none of them ages him

### The ladder

* beats equal BEATS exactly
* levels equal LEVELS exactly, plus one unnumbered epilogue of four beats
* every level's first beat opens with "Level <word>: <rank name>." in that
  exact shape, unvaried, and the rank name says what the job IS rather than
  giving a bare code like "GS-9" or "E-6"
* reading the labels alone in order gives a ladder a stranger could follow
* every level has all seven slots, in order, none skipped
* EVERY level carries a hard number
* reading the numbers alone in order, they escalate with no dip
* the number pattern breaks near the top and disappears at the very top
* every money figure is given in local currency AND rough dollars
* reading the costs alone in order, no two are the same kind of loss
* every rank name is the source's real name, verified, in the source's order
* the age is stated at level one and at three or four points after

### The threads

* exactly three threads, all seeded in level one
* each touched at least three times, changing each time
* all three paid off in the epilogue
* at least one thread closes a loop: you become the person from level one

### The epilogue

* four beats, unnumbered, titled in `plan.epilogue_title`
* the trap is an anaphora set of three, all set up earlier
* the reframe introduces no new information
* one line calls back to level one
* it does not moralise, list the levels, address the viewer, ask a question,
  or hand off to a successor
* nothing anywhere mentions the video, the channel or subscribing

### Prose

* every narration is 12 to 24 words, averaging near eighteen
* at least one beat in every level is under 16 words
* the final beat of the video is under 16 words
* every level label names a job, not a bare code or grade number
* every level contains a sentence of five words or fewer AND one of eighteen
  or more
* most beats end on a short tail clause, not on a flat restatement
* no more than one beat in four joins two clauses with a comma plus "and"
* every level names at least one physical object in the narration
* every level contains at least one anaphora set of three, landing inside a
  single beat
* no beat names an emotion the climber feels
* no banned word from Section 7 appears in any narration
* no word would make a fluent adult ESL speaker pause, unless it is a proper
  noun, title or named technical term translated in plain words on first use
* no quotation marks anywhere
* at least one beat in three carries a tag, all from the list, and no
  [whispers], [excited] or [laughs] is followed by an untagged beat

### Images

* four to six locations, none shorter than six beats, ranges cover every beat,
  every `location` string matches the plan character for character
* every image_prompt contains its location's exact `visual_anchor`
* `setting_anchor` names no building material, wall, roof or ground surface
  that any `visual_anchor` also names
* every image_prompt is 140 to 220 words, none over 260
* every image_prompt_alt is 170 to 260 words, none over 300
* no image_prompt uses negative construction or abstract nouns
* no image_prompt or anchor contains a century, a decade or an era name
* no person, group, building or environment is left as a bare noun
* every named organisation shows its visible signature
* every image_prompt names at least two saturated colours
* every uniform, building and piece of equipment is drawn the way the SOURCE
  draws it, with no real-world military or police kit substituted in
* no container is named by its contents and no surface is named by its function
* no two beats share an image_prompt
* every image_prompt_alt differs in BOTH ways: names removed AND staging changed
* every image_prompt_alt contains MORE physical description than its primary
* every primary that uses an official name also fully describes that element
* recurring characters carry the same core description in every beat
* every back-view prompt carries BACK_CLAUSE verbatim, and no other prompt does
* the frames climb in scale across the ladder as `visual_note` says
* no image prompt contains a style block, an art style or an aspect ratio

### Facts

* every entry in `plan.facts_to_check` is checked against the beats, and no
  beat contradicts one
* every invented number is marked `"invented": True`, holds the same value in
  every beat that uses it, and contradicts nothing the source states
* no invented rank, no renamed rank, no reordered ladder

### Booleans, checked by looking

* search the finished output for the exact strings `true` and `false` in
  lowercase. There must be zero of both.
* every boolean reads `True` or `False`, capitalised and unquoted
* no field anywhere holds `None`, `null` or `Null`

Then output the JSON, and nothing else.
